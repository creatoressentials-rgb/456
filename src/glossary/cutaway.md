<h2>Cutaway</h2>
<p>A <strong>cutaway</strong> is a supplementary shot that temporarily leaves the main action to show a related person, object, location, reaction, or event outside the primary framing. The edit then often returns to the main action.</p>
<p>Cutaways can provide context, reveal information, control pacing, hide an edit, illustrate narration, or redirect the audience's attention.</p>

<h3>Cutaway vs. Cut-In vs. B-Roll</h3>
<table><thead><tr><th>Term</th><th>What It Shows</th><th>Example</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Cutaway</td><td>Something related but outside the main action or primary frame</td><td>Interview subject speaks, then the edit shows a listener reacting</td></tr>
<tr><td>Cut-in</td><td>A closer view of something already within the main scene</td><td>Wide view of a repair, then a close-up of the screw being tightened</td></tr>
<tr><td>B-roll</td><td>Supplementary footage used to illustrate, add context, or cover edits</td><td>Exterior footage, process details, archive images, and cutaways</td></tr>
<tr><td>Insert</td><td>An isolated detail important to the scene</td><td>A close-up of a phone notification or product label</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>Cutaways can be B-roll, but B-roll is the broader category. A cutaway is defined by its relationship to the main action in the edit.</p>

<h3>What Can a Cutaway Show?</h3>
<ul>
<li>A listener's reaction during an interview</li>
<li>An object being discussed</li>
<li>A clock, sign, screen, or environmental detail</li>
<li>The audience at an event</li>
<li>A second activity occurring nearby</li>
<li>An exterior location during interior dialogue</li>
<li>Hands, tools, ingredients, or results related to narration</li>
<li>An archival photo, graphic, map, or screenshot</li>
</ul>

<h3>Why Creators Use Cutaways</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Hide edits:</strong> Cover a removed pause, mistake, or shortened answer.</li>
<li><strong>Show rather than tell:</strong> Let viewers see the object or process being described.</li>
<li><strong>Add context:</strong> Reveal where the action occurs or what surrounds it.</li>
<li><strong>Control pacing:</strong> Break up a long static interview or demonstration.</li>
<li><strong>Build meaning:</strong> Connect dialogue with a reaction, symbol, or related image.</li>
<li><strong>Maintain clarity:</strong> Show the result before returning to the instructor.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Cutaways in Talking-Head Videos</h3>
<p>In a <a href="/glossary/talking-head-video/">talking-head video</a>, a cutaway can cover a jump cut while the speaker's audio continues. Useful options include product footage, screenshots, documents, photographs, location footage, reactions, or a close-up demonstration.</p>
<p>The cutaway should support the current idea. Random stock footage may hide the edit but weaken trust or comprehension when it does not accurately represent what the speaker says.</p>

<h3>How to Plan Cutaways</h3>
<ol>
<li>Read the script or interview topics and mark claims that benefit from visual proof or context.</li>
<li>Add specific cutaway needs to the <a href="/glossary/shot-list/">shot list</a>.</li>
<li>Capture wide, medium, and detailed variations when useful.</li>
<li>Record clean beginnings and endings so the editor has room to cut.</li>
<li>Hold static shots long enough to be usable.</li>
<li>Record relevant natural sound when it adds information.</li>
<li>Label footage so the editor can find the correct subject quickly.</li>
</ol>

<h3>How Long Should a Cutaway Last?</h3>
<p>There is no universal duration. It should remain long enough for viewers to understand the image and short enough to preserve the intended pace.</p>
<p>A complex document or product control may need more time than a simple reaction. Readability, movement, narration, and the destination of the next cut all matter.</p>

<h3>Continuity and Cutaways</h3>
<p>Cutaways can conceal continuity problems, but they can also create new ones. A product should not change position, a person's clothing should not change unexpectedly, and a screen should not display a later step before the narration reaches it.</p>
<p>Capture cutaways during the same setup or document the state of props and actions carefully.</p>

<h3>Cutaways and Copyright</h3>
<p>A cutaway does not become legally reusable merely because it is brief. Footage, photographs, music, screenshots, and artwork may still require permission, a license, or a valid legal exception.</p>
<p>Use original material, properly licensed assets, public-domain material, or content you are legally entitled to include.</p>

<h3>Common Cutaway Mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Calling every piece of B-roll a cutaway.</li>
<li>Using unrelated footage solely to hide a jump cut.</li>
<li>Showing the wrong model, location, interface, or process.</li>
<li>Recording clips too short for the editor to use.</li>
<li>Creating continuity errors between the main action and supplementary shot.</li>
<li>Using a cutaway so long that viewers forget the main speaker or action.</li>
<li>Assuming brief copyrighted footage is automatically permitted.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently Asked Questions</h3>
<h4>Is a reaction shot a cutaway?</h4>
<p>It can be when the edit leaves the primary action or speaker to show another person's reaction.</p>
<h4>Is a cutaway the same as B-roll?</h4>
<p>No. B-roll is a broad category of supplementary footage. A cutaway is a specific editorial move away from the main action.</p>
<h4>Can a cutaway hide a jump cut?</h4>
<p>Yes. The main audio can continue while the image cuts to relevant supplementary footage, hiding the visual discontinuity.</p>
<h4>Do cutaways have to be filmed at the same time?</h4>
<p>No, but later footage must accurately match the topic, product, location, continuity, and rights requirements.</p>
