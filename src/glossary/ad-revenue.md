<h2>Ad Revenue</h2>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> Ad revenue is the money you earn specifically from ads YouTube runs on your videos — not memberships, not <a href="/glossary/super-chat/">Super Chat</a>, not YouTube Premium payouts. On standard long-form videos, YouTube pays monetizing creators <strong>55% of the ad revenue</strong> their videos generate; YouTube keeps the other 45%. It's paid out through AdSense for YouTube on a monthly cycle that typically lags 6–8 weeks behind the month you actually earned it.</p>

<h3>The revenue split</h3>
<p>When an advertiser pays to run an ad on your video, that payment gets split:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Long-form videos (over ~60 seconds):</strong> you get <strong>55%</strong>, YouTube keeps 45%.</li>
<li><strong><a href="/glossary/youtube-shorts/">YouTube Shorts</a>:</strong> different mechanics entirely. Shorts ad revenue isn't split per video — it's pooled. YouTube adds up all Shorts Feed ad revenue for the month into a Creator Pool, deducts music licensing costs if your Short used licensed tracks, then allocates your share based on your percentage of total <a href="/glossary/engaged-views/">engaged views</a> among monetizing creators in your country. You then keep 45% of whatever gets allocated to you. This is why Shorts RPM tends to look much lower than long-form RPM — it's not the same math.</li>
<li><strong>Memberships, Super Chat, <a href="/glossary/super-thanks/">Super Thanks</a>:</strong> a separate, unrelated 70/30 split in creators' favor — see <a href="/glossary/channel-memberships/">Channel Memberships</a>. Don't lump these into your "ad revenue" number; YouTube Analytics doesn't, and neither should you.</li>
</ul>
<p>This split has held steady at 55/45 for long-form since the Partner Program launched, and it isn't negotiable regardless of channel size.</p>

<h3>How it's actually paid out</h3>
<p>Ad revenue moves through <strong>AdSense for YouTube</strong>, on a fixed monthly cycle:</p>
<ol>
<li>You earn revenue throughout the month as ads run on your videos.</li>
<li>YouTube finalizes the month's earnings and posts them to your AdSense balance between the <strong>7th and 12th of the following month</strong>.</li>
<li>If your balance has hit the payment threshold (<strong>$100 in the US</strong>, varies by country) and there are no holds on your account, YouTube issues payment between the <strong>21st and 26th of that same month</strong>.</li>
<li>The money then takes a few more business days to actually land in your bank account.</li>
</ol>
<p>Net result: revenue you earn early in a month can take close to two months to reach your bank — commonly cited as around a 68-day average lag from earning to deposit. If you don't hit the threshold in a given month, the balance simply rolls forward — it doesn't disappear.</p>

<h3>What affects how much you actually earn</h3>
<p>Your ad revenue isn't just "views × a rate." It moves with:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong><a href="/glossary/niche/">Niche</a> and advertiser demand</strong> — finance, tech, and business content commands far higher CPMs than, say, gaming or vlogging, because advertisers in those categories pay more to reach the audience.</li>
<li><strong>Viewer geography</strong> — advertisers bid differently by country, so a view from the US or UK is typically worth several times more than a view from a lower-<a href="/glossary/cost-per-mille-cpm/">CPM</a> market.</li>
<li><strong>Season</strong> — CPMs climb in Q4 (holiday advertiser demand) and typically soften in January and summer.</li>
<li><strong>Ad blockers</strong> — a real and growing drag on monetized playbacks; blocked ads simply don't generate revenue, even if the view itself still counts.</li>
<li><strong>Video length and ad density</strong> — longer videos can carry more ad breaks (mid-rolls), but only if <a href="/glossary/retention/">retention</a> supports it. A 20-minute video that loses most viewers by minute 4 won't serve as many ads as the runtime suggests.</li>
<li><strong>Whether the view is actually monetized</strong> — not every view carries an ad. For the mechanics behind that distinction and how <a href="/glossary/youtube-cpm/">CPM</a> is actually calculated, see <a href="/glossary/monetized-playbacks/">Monetized Playbacks</a> and <a href="/glossary/playback-based-cpm/">Playback-Based CPM</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p>One common trap: your <strong>RPM</strong> (revenue per mille) in YouTube Analytics blends <em>all</em> revenue sources — ads, Premium, memberships, Super Chat — across <em>all</em> views, not just monetized ones. It's a useful health-check metric, but it is not your ad rate. See <a href="/glossary/youtube-rpm/">YouTube RPM</a> for the full breakdown.</p>

<h3>FAQ</h3>
<h4>Is ad revenue the same as my total YouTube earnings?</h4>
<p>No. Ad revenue is only the money from ads shown on your videos. Your total earnings can also include channel memberships, Super Chat, Super Thanks, and YouTube Premium watch-time payouts — each with its own separate revenue split.</p>
<h4>Why hasn't my ad revenue shown up in my bank account yet?</h4>
<p>Because of the payout cycle: earnings finalize 7th–12th of the next month, payment is issued 21st–26th of that month (only once you hit the $100 threshold), and bank transfer adds a few more days. It's normal for this to take close to two months from when you earned it.</p>
<h4>Does YouTube Shorts pay the same ad revenue rate as regular videos?</h4>
<p>No. Shorts revenue comes from a pooled Creator Pool based on your share of engaged views (after music licensing costs are deducted), and you keep 45% of your allocation — a fundamentally different mechanism than the straightforward 55/45 split on long-form ad revenue.</p>
