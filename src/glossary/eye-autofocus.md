<h2>Eye Autofocus</h2>
<p><strong>Eye Autofocus</strong>, often called <strong>Eye AF</strong>, is a camera feature that detects a subject's eye and places focus on or near that eye automatically.</p>
<p>For YouTubers, it is especially helpful during:</p>
<ul>
<li>Talking-head videos</li>
<li>Vlogging</li>
<li>Interviews</li>
<li>Livestreams</li>
<li>Tutorials</li>
<li>Product reviews</li>
<li>Thumbnail photography</li>
</ul>
<p>Because viewers naturally look at a person's eyes, accurate eye focus can make a face appear sharper and more engaging.</p>
<h3>Eye Autofocus vs. face detection</h3>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
  <th>Eye Autofocus</th>
  <th>Face detection</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
  <td>Targets an eye</td>
  <td>Targets the broader face</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>More precise with shallow focus</td>
  <td>Often more forgiving when eyes are hidden</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Useful for close talking heads</td>
  <td>Useful when the face is small</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>May select left or right eye</td>
  <td>Usually follows the whole face</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Can fail with glasses or side profiles</td>
  <td>May continue with head or body tracking</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>Many cameras combine these systems. If an eye cannot be detected, the camera may fall back to the face, head, or body.</p>
<h3>Eye AF and continuous autofocus</h3>
<p>Eye AF usually works best with continuous <a href="/glossary/autofocus/">autofocus</a>.</p>
<p>The camera repeatedly updates focus while the creator:</p>
<ul>
<li>Leans forward</li>
<li>Turns the head</li>
<li>Walks</li>
<li>Moves around the frame</li>
<li>Changes distance from the camera</li>
</ul>
<p>Single autofocus may detect the eye once and then stop updating.</p>
<h3>Why Eye AF matters for YouTubers</h3>
<h4>Shallow depth of field</h4>
<p>At a wide <a href="/glossary/aperture/">aperture</a>, only a narrow area may be sharp.</p>
<p>The nose can look sharp while the eye looks soft if focus lands slightly forward.</p>
<h4>Solo recording</h4>
<p>A creator cannot stand behind the camera and adjust focus while presenting.</p>
<p>Eye AF can reduce the need for a camera operator.</p>
<h4>Movement</h4>
<p>Creators often lean toward a desk, turn toward a monitor, or hold objects toward the lens.</p>
<p>Reliable tracking helps maintain focus through those movements.</p>
<h3>When Eye AF struggles</h3>
<ul>
<li>The face is very small</li>
<li>The eye is covered</li>
<li>The creator wears reflective glasses</li>
<li>Lighting is poor</li>
<li>The face is strongly backlit</li>
<li>The subject turns sideways</li>
<li>Hair covers the eye</li>
<li>The focus area excludes the face</li>
<li>The lens focuses slowly</li>
<li>The creator is closer than the lens's minimum focus distance</li>
</ul>
<h3>Left-eye and right-eye priority</h3>
<p>Some cameras allow:</p>
<ul>
<li>Automatic eye selection</li>
<li>Left-eye priority</li>
<li>Right-eye priority</li>
<li>Touch selection</li>
</ul>
<p>Automatic selection is convenient. Manual selection can help when a face is angled and the creator wants the nearest eye sharp.</p>
<h3>Eye AF during product demonstrations</h3>
<p>A creator may hold a product in front of the face.</p>
<p>The camera can:</p>
<ul>
<li>Stay on the eye</li>
<li>Switch to the product</li>
<li>Pulse between both</li>
<li>Lose the subject</li>
</ul>
<p>Creator-focused cameras may offer a product-showcase mode. Test this before recording.</p>
<h3>Eye AF settings to test</h3>
<ul>
<li>Human subject detection</li>
<li>Continuous AF</li>
<li>Eye priority</li>
<li>Focus area</li>
<li>Tracking sensitivity</li>
<li>Transition speed</li>
<li>Product mode</li>
<li>Left/right eye selection</li>
<li>Face registration, if available</li>
</ul>
<h3>Eye AF and lens choice</h3>
<p>Performance depends partly on the lens.</p>
<p>Consider:</p>
<ul>
<li>Focus-motor speed</li>
<li>Focus-motor noise</li>
<li>Maximum aperture</li>
<li>Minimum focus distance</li>
<li>Firmware compatibility</li>
<li>Focus breathing</li>
</ul>
<p>A camera may recognize the eye correctly while a slow lens takes too long to reach focus.</p>
<h3>Practical self-recording test</h3>
<ol>
<li>Sit in the normal position.</li>
<li>Lean toward and away from the camera.</li>
<li>Turn both directions.</li>
<li>Cover one eye.</li>
<li>Put on glasses.</li>
<li>Hold a product in front of the face.</li>
<li>Leave and re-enter the frame.</li>
<li>Review footage at full size.</li>
</ol>
<p>A focus box on the screen does not always prove the recorded eye is perfectly sharp.</p>
<h3>Common mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Assuming Eye AF works in every recording mode</li>
<li>Using single AF for a moving presenter</li>
<li>Opening the lens too wide</li>
<li>Ignoring minimum focus distance</li>
<li>Confusing face detection with eye detection</li>
<li>Failing to test glasses and side profiles</li>
<li>Trusting the screen without reviewing footage</li>
<li>Ignoring lens noise</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Is Eye Autofocus better than face detection?</h4>
<p>It is more precise when the eye is visible, but face detection can be more reliable when the subject is small or partly hidden.</p>
<h4>Does Eye AF work for video?</h4>
<p>Many modern cameras support Eye AF during video, but availability and behavior vary by model and recording mode.</p>
<h4>Why does Eye AF focus on the wrong eye?</h4>
<p>The camera may select the nearest or clearest eye. Some models allow left-eye or right-eye priority.</p>
<h4>Do YouTubers need Eye Autofocus?</h4>
<p>It is not required, but it is highly useful for solo creators who move or record with shallow depth of field.</p>
