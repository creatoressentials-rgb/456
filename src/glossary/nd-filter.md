<h2>ND Filter</h2>
<p>An <strong>ND filter</strong>, or <strong>neutral density filter</strong>, reduces the amount of light entering a camera without intentionally changing the scene's color. It lets creators use a wider <a href="/glossary/aperture">aperture</a>, slower <a href="/glossary/shutter-speed">shutter speed</a>, or preferred <a href="/glossary/iso">ISO</a> in conditions that would otherwise overexpose the image.</p>
<p>For video, an ND filter is especially useful outdoors. It allows the creator to keep a natural motion-blur shutter setting and a chosen <a href="/glossary/depth-of-field">depth of field</a> instead of closing the aperture or using an extremely fast shutter merely because the sunlight is bright.</p>
<h3>What an ND filter does</h3>
<p>An ND filter absorbs or blocks part of the incoming light before it reaches the lens or sensor. A well-made filter aims to reduce visible wavelengths evenly, which is why it is called <strong>neutral</strong> density.</p>
<p>The filter does not make the scene darker in reality. It reduces the exposure recorded by the camera.</p>
<p>Creators use that reduction to control:</p>
<ul>
<li>Shutter speed and motion blur.</li>
<li>Aperture and background separation.</li>
<li>Exposure in bright environments.</li>
<li>Long-exposure photography.</li>
<li>Flash balance and synchronization.</li>
<li>Consistency when moving between lighting conditions.</li>
</ul>
<p>An ND filter does not create additional <a href="/glossary/dynamic-range">dynamic range</a>. It may help prevent highlight clipping by reducing exposure, but it cannot expand the sensor's underlying tonal capacity.</p>

<p>An "ND8" filter usually means a filter factor of eight, equal to three stops. An "ND 0.8" label could be interpreted differently and is not a standard one-stop increment. Product names are not perfectly consistent, so verify the listed <strong>stop reduction</strong> or <strong>percentage transmission</strong> rather than relying on the number alone.</p>
<h3>Fixed ND filters</h3>
<p>A <strong>fixed ND filter</strong> has one density. Common strengths include one, two, three, six, and ten stops.</p>
<p>Advantages include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Predictable exposure reduction.</li>
<li>No rotating adjustment to bump accidentally.</li>
<li>Often more consistent color and density.</li>
<li>Less risk of polarization artifacts.</li>
<li>Easy matching across several cameras when calibrated.</li>
</ul>
<p>Disadvantages include carrying and changing multiple filters. Stacking fixed filters can increase reflections, vignetting, flare, and color shifts, especially with wide lenses.</p>
<h3>Variable ND filters</h3>
<p>A <strong>variable ND filter</strong>, or VND, changes density as the front ring rotates. It uses a polarizing design to provide several strengths in one filter.</p>
<p>Advantages include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Fast exposure adjustment.</li>
<li>Fewer filters to carry.</li>
<li>Smooth changes for run-and-gun video.</li>
<li>Convenient control when light changes quickly.</li>
</ul>
<p>Limitations can include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Color shifts at different positions.</li>
<li>Uneven darkening or an X-shaped pattern near the strongest setting.</li>
<li>Unwanted changes to reflections, skies, screens, or skin because of polarization.</li>
<li>Vignetting on wide lenses.</li>
<li>Reference marks that are not calibrated exact stops.</li>
</ul>
<p>Stay within the manufacturer's recommended range. If the image begins to show uneven density, back the filter away from its extreme setting or switch to a stronger range.</p>
<h3>Graduated ND filters</h3>
<p>A <strong>graduated ND filter</strong> transitions from dark to clear. It is designed to reduce one part of the frame, such as a bright sky, while leaving another part less affected.</p>
<p>Graduated filters are most useful when the brightness boundary is relatively predictable. They are less convenient when subjects cross the transition or when trees, buildings, and people extend into the darkened area.</p>
<p>Digital blending and masking can replace some graduated-filter uses, but a filter can prevent bright regions from clipping before the image is recorded.</p>
<h3>IRND filters</h3>
<p>Strong ND filtration can reveal differences between visible-light and infrared response. Some cameras may show contaminated blacks or color shifts when heavy filtration reduces visible light more effectively than infrared.</p>
<p>An <strong>IRND filter</strong> is designed to control infrared as well as visible light. This is especially relevant to cinema cameras, strong ND levels, dark fabrics, and cameras with weaker internal infrared filtration.</p>
<p>Not every creator needs IRND. Test the specific camera and filter combination before an important production.</p>
<h3>Built-in ND filters</h3>
<p>Some compact, cinema, broadcast, and professional video cameras include internal ND filters. These may be:</p>
<ul>
<li>Fixed filters that move into the optical path.</li>
<li>A wheel with several strengths.</li>
<li>Electronic variable ND systems with smooth adjustment.</li>
</ul>
<p>Built-in ND is fast and avoids attaching front filters, but the available strengths and operation differ by camera. A built-in filter is still optical light control; it is not the same as lowering ISO electronically.</p>
<h3>Why video creators use ND filters</h3>
<p>Video creators often choose shutter speed from the <a href="/glossary/frame-rate-fps">frame rate</a>. A common guideline uses a shutter duration near twice the frame-rate denominator: around 1/50 for 24 or 25 fps, 1/60 for 30 fps, and 1/120 or 1/125 for 60 fps.</p>
<p>In daylight, those settings can admit too much light at a wide aperture. Without ND, the creator may have to:</p>
<ul>
<li>Close the lens and lose shallow depth of field.</li>
<li>Use a very fast shutter and create crisp, staccato motion.</li>
<li>Lower ISO below the camera's preferred base setting.</li>
<li>Accept clipped highlights.</li>
</ul>
<p>An ND filter reduces light while preserving the intended creative settings.</p>
<h3>ND filters for photography</h3>
<p>Photographers use ND filters to create longer exposures in bright conditions. Examples include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Smoothing moving water.</li>
<li>Turning traffic or people into motion trails.</li>
<li>Blurring clouds.</li>
<li>Removing moving crowds from long exposures.</li>
<li>Using wide apertures with flash in daylight.</li>
<li>Staying within a camera's flash-sync limit.</li>
</ul>
<p>A tripod, remote release, and careful light-leak control become increasingly important with strong filters and long exposure times.</p>

<h3>Choosing an ND filter</h3>
<p>Consider:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Stop range:</strong> Light outdoor video may need two to five stops; harsh sun and wide apertures may require more.</li>
<li><strong>Lens diameter:</strong> Match the thread size or use step-up rings.</li>
<li><strong>Wide-angle coverage:</strong> Thin rings and larger outer elements can reduce vignetting.</li>
<li><strong>Color consistency:</strong> Poor filters may create green, magenta, warm, or cool casts.</li>
<li><strong>Sharpness:</strong> Low-quality glass or resin can soften detail.</li>
<li><strong>Coatings:</strong> Good coatings reduce flare, reflections, fingerprints, and water adhesion.</li>
<li><strong>Hard stops:</strong> A limited variable range can prevent rotation into unusable cross-polarization.</li>
<li><strong>Filter system:</strong> Screw-in filters are compact; square filters and matte boxes are faster for lens changes.</li>
<li><strong>Infrared control:</strong> Strong filtration on some cinema cameras may benefit from IRND.</li>
</ol>
<p>Creators with several lenses often buy one quality filter in the largest needed diameter and use step-up rings on smaller lenses.</p>
<h3>How to use an ND filter for video</h3>
<ol>
<li>Choose the recording <a href="/glossary/frame-rate-fps">frame rate</a>.</li>
<li>Set the desired shutter speed or shutter angle.</li>
<li>Choose the aperture for the intended <a href="/glossary/depth-of-field">depth of field</a>.</li>
<li>Set the camera's appropriate ISO or profile base.</li>
<li>Add or rotate ND until exposure is correct.</li>
<li>Check a waveform, zebras, or histogram for clipping.</li>
<li>Confirm that the filter has not created an unwanted color shift, X pattern, or reflection change.</li>
<li>Lock the filter position before recording when consistency matters.</li>
</ol>
<p>ND should support the creative exposure decision, not replace exposure monitoring.</p>
<h3>Common ND filter mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Treating every "ND8" or "ND 0.8" label as the same strength.</li>
<li>Rotating a variable ND beyond its usable range.</li>
<li>Stacking many filters and ignoring flare or vignetting.</li>
<li>Buying a small filter for one lens instead of planning a step-up-ring system.</li>
<li>Assuming "neutral" means every filter is perfectly colorless.</li>
<li>Using ND to compensate for poor lighting ratios indoors.</li>
<li>Forgetting that variable ND can change reflections and display screens.</li>
<li>Leaving a strong filter attached in low light and raising ISO unnecessarily.</li>
<li>Believing ND increases sensor dynamic range.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>What strength ND filter do I need for video?</h4>
<p>It depends on sunlight, aperture, ISO, and frame rate. A two-to-five-stop variable filter handles many moderate conditions, while wide-aperture shooting in harsh sun may require six to nine stops. Use exposure tools rather than a universal preset.</p>
<h4>Is a variable ND better than fixed ND?</h4>
<p>It is faster and more convenient, but a fixed filter can provide more predictable density and fewer polarization artifacts. Many creators use variable ND for fast work and fixed or internal ND for controlled productions.</p>
<h4>Can I leave an ND filter on all the time?</h4>
<p>You can, but it is usually inefficient in dim light. The filter forces a wider aperture, slower shutter, or higher ISO and adds another optical surface that can collect dust, flare, or reflections.</p>
<h4>Does an ND filter affect image quality?</h4>
<p>Any filter can. A quality model minimizes sharpness loss, color shift, flare, vignetting, and uneven density. Test strong and variable filters on the widest lens and highest-resolution mode you intend to use.</p>
