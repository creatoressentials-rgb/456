<h2>Stayed to Watch</h2>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> Stayed to Watch is a <a href="/glossary/youtube-shorts/">YouTube Shorts</a> metric that shows the percentage of viewers who kept watching a Short past its opening seconds instead of swiping to the next one. You'll find it in <a href="/glossary/youtube-studio/">YouTube Studio</a> under <strong>Analytics → Content → Shorts</strong>, in the "How viewers engaged" section. It's YouTube's official term for what creators commonly call "viewed vs. swiped away" — it's your Short's hook rate.</p>

<h3>What it means, exactly</h3>
<p>YouTube defines Stayed to Watch as "the percentage of times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short." It only applies to Shorts — regular videos and live streams don't have this metric, because the vertical, swipe-to-skip feed is what makes the first couple of seconds so decisive.</p>
<p>It's closely tied to <a href="/glossary/engaged-views/">Engaged Views</a>, another Shorts-specific metric. The relationship is simple: <strong>Engaged Views is the raw count</strong> of viewers who stayed past the initial seconds; <strong>Stayed to Watch is that same behavior expressed as a percentage</strong> of everyone who saw the Short. Same underlying signal, two different formats.</p>
<p>From there, YouTube calculates <a href="/glossary/average-view-duration/">average view duration</a> and <a href="/glossary/average-percentage-viewed/">average percentage viewed</a> for your Short — but only from the pool of viewers who already stayed to watch. So the order matters:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Shown in feed</strong> — your Short gets served</li>
<li><strong>Stayed to Watch / Engaged Views</strong> — did they stop scrolling?</li>
<li><strong>Average view duration / % viewed</strong> — did they stick around once they stopped?</li>
<li><strong>Satisfaction signals</strong> — likes, dislikes, "Not interested," survey responses</li>
</ol>

<h3>Is Stayed to Watch the same as Audience <a href="/glossary/retention/">Retention</a>?</h3>
<p>No, and this is where a lot of creators get confused. The "Audience retention" report with its intro percentage, spikes, and dips is built for regular videos. Shorts don't get that same graph — instead, Shorts get Stayed to Watch as a single hook-rate number, plus average view duration and average % viewed as separate hold-rate numbers. Think of Stayed to Watch as the gate a viewer has to pass through before retention (in the traditional sense) even becomes relevant.</p>

<h3>Where to find it in YouTube Studio</h3>
<ol>
<li>Open <strong>YouTube Studio</strong></li>
<li>Go to <strong>Analytics</strong></li>
<li>Click the <strong>Content</strong> tab</li>
<li>Select the <strong>Shorts</strong> chip</li>
<li>Look for <strong>"How viewers engaged"</strong> — that percentage is Stayed to Watch</li>
</ol>
<p>Note: you may also see this same concept referred to elsewhere as "Viewed (vs. Swiped Away)" or "how many chose to view." These are informal or older labels for the same thing — not separate metrics.</p>

<h3>Why it matters</h3>
<p>YouTube tests new Shorts on a small "seed" audience before deciding whether to push them further. Stayed to Watch is one of the earliest signals in that decision — a Short that gets swiped away quickly by its first viewers is less likely to get distributed to more people, while one that holds attention tends to get shown more.</p>
<p>A word of caution: a lot of creator content online cites specific benchmark percentages (like "70% is good, 50% is average") as if YouTube has published them. <strong>YouTube has not published an official benchmark for Stayed to Watch.</strong> Treat any specific number you see quoted online as informal creator consensus, not platform fact. The one comparison YouTube itself supports is checking your Short's performance against your own similar-length uploads inside Studio — that's the only benchmark grounded in your actual data.</p>

<h3>How to improve it</h3>
<p>Since Stayed to Watch is decided in the first couple of seconds, the fix is almost entirely about your opening, not your whole video:</p>
<ul>
<li>Make the first frame communicate what the Short is about without sound</li>
<li>Open mid-action or on the payoff — don't build up to it</li>
<li>Avoid slow intros, greetings, or context-setting before the hook</li>
<li>Make sure the opening promise matches what the video actually delivers (mismatches show up later as drop-off in average % viewed, even if Stayed to Watch looks fine)</li>
</ul>

<h3>FAQ</h3>
<h4>Is Stayed to Watch the same as Engaged Views?</h4>
<p>No, but they measure the same behavior. Engaged Views is the count of viewers who stayed past the initial seconds; Stayed to Watch is that same group shown as a percentage of everyone who was served the Short.</p>
<h4>What's a good Stayed to Watch percentage?</h4>
<p>YouTube hasn't published an official target. Numbers you'll see floating around online (like "70%+") are informal creator benchmarks, not platform data. Use YouTube Studio's built-in comparison against your own similar-length Shorts as your real baseline.</p>
<h4>Does Stayed to Watch affect whether my Short gets recommended?</h4>
<p>YouTube tests Shorts on a small audience first and uses early viewer behavior to decide whether to expand distribution, so a strong Stayed to Watch rate is generally a good early signal. YouTube hasn't published the exact weighting, so treat this as directional, not a guaranteed ranking factor.</p>
