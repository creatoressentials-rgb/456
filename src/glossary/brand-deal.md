<h2>Brand Deal</h2>
<p>A <strong>brand deal</strong> is a commercial partnership in which a creator produces, publishes, licenses, or promotes content for a company in exchange for money or another benefit. It is also commonly called a <strong>brand <a href="/glossary/sponsorship/">sponsorship</a></strong>, <strong>creator sponsorship</strong>, <strong>paid partnership</strong>, or <strong>branded-content deal</strong>.</p>
<p>The arrangement may involve a short mention inside a YouTube video, a dedicated review, a Short, a livestream segment, licensed creator footage, event attendance, affiliate compensation, or a longer ambassador relationship. The exact <a href="/glossary/deliverables/">deliverables</a>, payment, rights, deadlines, and <a href="/glossary/approval-process/">approval process</a> should be defined before work begins.</p>
<p>YouTube describes a brand deal as a partnership between creators and brands. The resulting videos or media assets are commonly called <strong>branded content</strong>.</p>
<h3>What counts as compensation in a brand deal?</h3>
<p>A brand deal is not limited to a cash payment. A creator may receive:</p>
<ul>
<li>A flat production or publishing fee</li>
<li>Free products or services</li>
<li>Travel, lodging, tickets, or event access</li>
<li>Affiliate commissions</li>
<li>Performance bonuses</li>
<li>A licensing fee for the creator’s content or likeness</li>
<li>Revenue sharing</li>
<li>Equity or another financial interest</li>
<li>A discount or benefit not available to the public</li>
</ul>
<p>A free product can create a material connection even when the creator is not paid cash. The disclosure question depends on the relationship and the reasonable expectations surrounding the product—not only the dollar amount.</p>
<h3>Common types of creator brand deals</h3>
<h4>Integrated sponsorship</h4>
<p>The creator includes a sponsored segment inside a broader video. The brand mention may last from a few seconds to several minutes and should be clearly distinguished from the creator’s unsponsored content.</p>
<h4>Dedicated <a href="/glossary/sponsored-content/">sponsored content</a></h4>
<p>The entire video, Short, post, or livestream is built around the sponsor, product, or campaign. Dedicated content generally requires more production time and gives the brand greater prominence.</p>
<h4>Product placement</h4>
<p>The brand or product appears directly in the content. YouTube defines paid product placement as content created for a third party in exchange for compensation where that party’s brand, message, or product is integrated into the content.</p>
<h4>Endorsement</h4>
<p>The creator expresses an opinion, recommendation, or experience that viewers are likely to understand as the creator’s own. Endorsements must be truthful and cannot claim experience the creator did not have.</p>
<h4>Sponsorship</h4>
<p>A third party finances the content in whole or in part and receives promotion. YouTube distinguishes sponsorships from direct product placement, although creators and advertisers often use the terms loosely.</p>
<h4><a href="/glossary/content-licensing/">Content licensing</a> or creator partnerships boost</h4>
<p>A brand may pay to use the creator’s video in advertising, on its own channels, or in other media. On YouTube, a creator can grant brand partner access that may allow the brand to view certain performance data and promote the video through Google Ads. <a href="/glossary/usage-rights/">Usage rights</a> should be priced and documented separately from ordinary organic posting.</p>
<h4>Affiliate or performance-based deal</h4>
<p>The creator earns a commission for tracked sales, leads, installs, or another action. <a href="/glossary/affiliate-marketing/">Affiliate marketing</a> can stand alone or be combined with a guaranteed sponsorship fee.</p>

<p>A deal can use both models, such as a $2,000 integration plus a sales commission. The contract should explain whether commissions are additional compensation, replace part of the fee, or depend on a minimum threshold.</p>
<h3>What a brand deal should define</h3>
<p>A professional agreement normally addresses:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Deliverables:</strong> Number, platform, format, duration, and placement of the content</li>
<li><strong>Schedule:</strong> Draft, review, publication, and reporting dates</li>
<li><strong>Compensation:</strong> Fee, currency, deposit, <a href="/glossary/payment-terms/">payment terms</a>, expenses, and bonuses</li>
<li><strong>Creative direction:</strong> Required messages, prohibited claims, product facts, tone, and call to action</li>
<li><strong>Review process:</strong> Who can request revisions, how many rounds are included, and what can be changed</li>
<li><strong>Usage rights:</strong> Where, how, and how long the brand may reuse the content</li>
<li><strong>Paid amplification:</strong> Whether the brand can turn the creator’s content into advertising</li>
<li><strong>Exclusivity:</strong> Competitors the creator cannot work with and the restricted period</li>
<li><strong>Content availability:</strong> How long the sponsored content must remain public</li>
<li><strong>Disclosure and compliance:</strong> Platform declarations, viewer-facing disclosures, and claim substantiation</li>
<li><strong>Cancellation:</strong> Kill fees, rescheduling, product delays, and what happens if the campaign stops</li>
<li><strong>Ownership:</strong> Who owns footage, project files, <a href="/glossary/youtube-thumbnails/">thumbnails</a>, scripts, and derivative versions</li>
</ul>
<p>The <a href="/glossary/campaign-brief/">campaign brief</a> and <a href="/glossary/creative-brief/">creative brief</a> provide direction, but the contract establishes the legally binding business terms. A brief should not be assumed to replace a signed agreement.</p>
<h3>How creators find brand deals</h3>
<p>Creators may receive inbound offers or pitch brands directly. Common paths include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Contact information listed on the channel or <a href="/glossary/creator-media-kit/">creator media kit</a></li>
<li>Direct outreach to brand, agency, or influencer-marketing teams</li>
<li>Talent managers and creator agencies</li>
<li>Creator marketplaces</li>
<li>Existing affiliate relationships</li>
<li>Referrals from other creators</li>
<li>YouTube <a href="/glossary/youtube-creator-partnerships/">Creator Partnerships</a></li>
<li>Brand partner access and channel-insight sharing in <a href="/glossary/youtube-studio/">YouTube Studio</a></li>
</ul>
<p>YouTube’s current Creator Partnerships tools can help brands discover creators and send inquiries. However, YouTube explains that creators generally negotiate and sign directly with the brand and are paid by the brand.</p>
<h3>How to evaluate a brand deal</h3>
<p>A large headline fee can still be a poor deal if the rights or obligations are excessive. Before accepting, evaluate:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong><a href="/glossary/audience-fit/">Audience fit</a>:</strong> Would the product genuinely help or interest the channel’s viewers?</li>
<li><strong>Creator credibility:</strong> Can the creator make an honest recommendation based on real experience?</li>
<li><strong>Workload:</strong> Include planning, production, revisions, posting, reporting, and administration.</li>
<li><strong>Expected performance:</strong> Use typical recent results, not only subscriber count or one <a href="/glossary/viral/">viral</a> video.</li>
<li><strong>Usage and exclusivity:</strong> Paid ads and category restrictions can be worth more than the original post.</li>
<li><strong>Payment risk:</strong> Confirm the legal entity, contract, payment method, and payment <a href="/glossary/timeline/">timeline</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Claim risk:</strong> Be cautious with medical, financial, earnings, environmental, or comparative claims.</li>
<li><strong>Long-term value:</strong> A repeat partner may be more valuable than an unrelated one-time campaign.</li>
</ol>
<p>Creators should verify unusual offers, avoid downloading unexpected files, and be suspicious of domains or payment requests that do not match the claimed company.</p>
<h3>Brand-deal disclosure on YouTube</h3>
<p>YouTube requires creators to identify videos that contain paid product placement, sponsorship, endorsement, or another commercial relationship by selecting the paid-promotion declaration when applicable. YouTube can then display a paid-promotion label and apply relevant platform policies.</p>
<p>That platform declaration is not necessarily the entire disclosure. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission says a material relationship should be disclosed clearly and conspicuously with the endorsement itself. For video, the FTC says the disclosure should appear in the video rather than only in the description and notes that viewers are more likely to notice disclosure made both visually and audibly.</p>
<p>The FTC also warns creators not to assume a platform’s built-in disclosure tool is sufficient by itself. Simple terms such as <strong>ad</strong>, <strong>advertisement</strong>, or <strong>sponsored</strong> are generally clearer than vague words such as <strong>collab</strong>, <strong>sp</strong>, or <strong>thanks</strong>.</p>
<p>Disclosure rules differ by country. Creators and brands are responsible for the laws, platform rules, and industry requirements that apply to their campaign.</p>
<h3>Brand deal vs. unpaid product seeding</h3>
<p>Product seeding means a brand sends a product, sometimes without a guaranteed posting requirement. That does not automatically make the resulting coverage independent.</p>
<p>Ask:</p>
<ul>
<li>Was the product requested or accepted under an agreement?</li>
<li>Did the brand expect, encourage, or require coverage?</li>
<li>Can the creator keep the product?</li>
<li>Is there an <a href="/glossary/affiliate-link/">affiliate link</a>, discount code, or future business relationship?</li>
<li>Would viewers consider the connection important when evaluating the recommendation?</li>
</ul>
<p>When the relationship could affect how viewers interpret the content, transparent disclosure is the safer and more credible approach.</p>
<h3>Common brand-deal mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Starting production without a signed agreement:</strong> Important rights and payment terms remain unresolved.</li>
<li><strong>Pricing only by subscriber count:</strong> Views, audience fit, workload, rights, and exclusivity also matter.</li>
<li><strong>Giving away broad usage rights:</strong> “In perpetuity, worldwide, all media” can be far more valuable than one organic post.</li>
<li><strong>Accepting unlimited revisions:</strong> The creator can become trapped in an open-ended approval cycle.</li>
<li><strong>Making claims supplied by the brand without support:</strong> The creator remains responsible for truthful endorsements.</li>
<li><strong>Hiding the disclosure:</strong> A vague label at the end of a description may be missed.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring competing sponsorships:</strong> Poorly planned integrations can conflict with other contracts or audience trust.</li>
<li><strong>Publishing before payment details are confirmed:</strong> Payment terms should be clear before the content goes live.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Is a brand deal the same as a sponsorship?</h4>
<p>Usually. In creator marketing, the terms are often used interchangeably. YouTube also describes a brand deal as a partnership that may be called a brand sponsorship.</p>
<h4>Can a small creator get brand deals?</h4>
<p>Yes. Brands may value <a href="/glossary/niche/">niche</a> authority, audience fit, trust, production skill, or consistent performance more than a large subscriber total.</p>
<h4>Does receiving a free product count as a brand deal?</h4>
<p>It can. A free product or other benefit may create a material connection, especially when the brand expects promotion or the creator keeps something of value.</p>
<h4>Does YouTube pay creators for every Creator Partnerships deal?</h4>
<p>No. YouTube’s current Help documentation says creators negotiate and sign directly with the brand and are paid by the brand for these opportunities.</p>
<h4>Does the YouTube paid-promotion box satisfy the FTC by itself?</h4>
<p>Not necessarily. The FTC says creators should not assume a platform tool is adequate and recommends a clear disclosure with the endorsement itself.</p>
