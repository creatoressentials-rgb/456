<h2>Exposure Triangle</h2>
<p>The <strong>exposure triangle</strong> is a teaching model that explains how <a href="/glossary/aperture">aperture</a>, shutter speed, and <a href="/glossary/iso">ISO</a> work together to determine recorded brightness and the visual character of an image.</p>
<p>The triangle is useful because changing one setting often requires compensation from another. A creator who chooses a faster shutter speed to freeze motion may need a wider aperture, a higher ISO, more light, or some combination of those changes to maintain the intended exposure.</p>
<h3>The three sides of the exposure triangle</h3>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
  <th>Setting</th>
  <th>Primary job</th>
  <th>Brighter direction</th>
  <th>Major visual tradeoff</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
  <td>Aperture</td>
  <td>Changes the lens opening</td>
  <td>Lower f-number and wider opening</td>
  <td>Changes <a href="/glossary/depth-of-field">depth of field</a> and lens performance</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Shutter speed or shutter duration</td>
  <td>Changes how long each frame gathers light</td>
  <td>Longer duration or slower shutter</td>
  <td>Changes motion blur and can affect flicker</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>ISO</td>
  <td>Changes signal amplification or brightness mapping</td>
  <td>Higher ISO value</td>
  <td>Can increase visible noise and reduce highlight headroom</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>The model is sometimes simplified by saying that all three settings control how much light enters the camera. That is not technically precise for digital cameras. Aperture and shutter duration change the light captured by the sensor. ISO generally changes how the captured signal is amplified or interpreted after capture.</p>
<p>ISO still belongs in the triangle because it changes recorded brightness and affects the settings needed for a usable image.</p>
<h3>What is exposure?</h3>
<p>Exposure describes how much light the camera records and how that information is rendered into an image. An underexposed recording is darker than intended and may contain noisy or lost shadows. An overexposed recording is brighter than intended and may contain clipped highlights.</p>
<p>The camera meter usually treats a certain average tone as its target. That target is not a creative command. A dark concert scene, bright snowfield, silhouette, or moody studio setup may need intentional compensation because the meter does not understand what the scene is supposed to look like.</p>
<p>A useful exposure protects important detail while serving the creator's visual goal.</p>
<h3>Stops and equivalent exposure</h3>
<p>A <strong>stop</strong> represents a doubling or halving of light or recorded brightness. The three triangle settings can be adjusted in stop-based increments.</p>
<p>For example, suppose a shot is exposed at:</p>
<ul>
<li>f/4</li>
<li>1/50 second</li>
<li>ISO 400</li>
</ul>
<p>Closing the aperture from f/4 to f/5.6 reduces light by one stop. The creator could compensate by changing ISO 400 to ISO 800 or by lengthening the shutter duration from 1/50 to approximately 1/25 second.</p>
<p>These combinations may produce similar brightness, but they do not produce the same image. F/5.6 increases depth of field. ISO 800 may show more noise or less highlight headroom. A 1/25 shutter creates more motion blur.</p>
<p>That is the real value of the exposure triangle: it teaches creators to balance brightness <strong>and</strong> side effects.</p>
<h3>How aperture affects exposure</h3>
<p>Aperture changes the size of the opening in the lens.</p>
<ul>
<li>A lower f-number such as f/1.8 represents a wider opening and more light.</li>
<li>A higher f-number such as f/8 represents a narrower opening and less light.</li>
<li>Wider apertures generally make shallow focus easier.</li>
<li>Narrower apertures generally increase depth of field but may require more light or ISO.</li>
</ul>
<p>For a talking-head video, creators often choose aperture based on how much movement the speaker makes. An extremely wide aperture can look attractive while making focus fragile. A moderate setting such as f/2.8 to f/5.6 may provide better consistency.</p>
<h3>How shutter speed affects exposure</h3>
<p>Shutter speed controls how long the camera gathers light for each frame. A longer exposure records more light and more motion blur. A shorter exposure records less light and freezes movement more sharply.</p>
<p>In video, shutter speed is tied closely to <a href="/glossary/frame-rate-fps">frame rate</a>. A common starting point is a shutter duration near half the frame interval, often called the 180-degree shutter guideline:</p>
<ul>
<li>24 fps: about 1/48 or 1/50 second</li>
<li>30 fps: about 1/60 second</li>
<li>60 fps: about 1/120 or 1/125 second</li>
</ul>
<p>This is a visual convention, not a platform requirement. Faster shutter speeds can suit sports, action, frame extraction, or deliberately crisp motion. Slower shutter speeds can create more blur but may look smeared and cannot exceed the time available for each frame.</p>
<p>Creators also need to consider light flicker. Artificial lighting and displays may pulse at frequencies related to local electrical power or their own refresh systems. Slight shutter adjustments can reduce visible bands or brightness changes.</p>
<h3>How ISO affects exposure</h3>
<p>ISO changes how strongly the camera amplifies or maps the sensor signal. Raising ISO makes the recorded image brighter without increasing the amount of light captured.</p>
<p>That distinction matters. A high ISO can make an underlit subject visible, but it cannot replace missing photons. If the signal is weak, amplification raises both the subject information and the noise.</p>
<p>A practical approach is to:</p>
<ol>
<li>Add or improve light when possible.</li>
<li>Choose shutter speed for motion.</li>
<li>Choose aperture for depth of field and lens behavior.</li>
<li>Raise ISO only as much as necessary for the intended exposure.</li>
</ol>
<p>Some cameras have base, native, or dual-base ISO behavior that changes the best choice. Camera-specific testing is more reliable than the blanket rule to always use the lowest number.</p>
<h3>The exposure triangle for video creators</h3>
<p>Photography guides often allow shutter speed to move freely. Video creators usually have less flexibility because shutter speed strongly affects motion continuity from frame to frame.</p>
<p>A useful video priority order is:</p>
<h4>1. Choose the frame rate</h4>
<p>Select 24, 25, 30, 50, 60, or another rate based on delivery, motion style, region, and slow-motion needs.</p>
<h4>2. Choose the motion rendering</h4>
<p>Set shutter speed or shutter angle. Start near the 180-degree convention when natural motion blur is desired, then adjust intentionally.</p>
<h4>3. Choose the depth of field</h4>
<p>Set aperture based on focus tolerance, subject separation, lens sharpness, and composition.</p>
<h4>4. Control the light</h4>
<p>Add, move, dim, diffuse, or block lights. Outdoors, use a neutral-density filter when the scene is too bright for the chosen shutter and aperture.</p>
<h4>5. Set ISO</h4>
<p>Use the ISO that produces a clean, properly placed signal while preserving needed highlight detail.</p>
<p>This order keeps creative decisions from being sacrificed merely to make the meter reach zero.</p>

<p>These are workflows rather than universal numeric presets.</p>
<h3>Exposure triangle versus exposure compensation</h3>
<p>Exposure compensation tells an automatic or semi-automatic camera mode to make the image brighter or darker than its meter's default target. The camera then adjusts one or more triangle settings according to the selected mode.</p>
<p>For example, in aperture priority the creator chooses the aperture, and the camera may change shutter speed and ISO. Adding negative exposure compensation tells the system to deliver a darker result.</p>
<p>Exposure compensation does not create an additional side of the triangle. It changes the target used by the camera's automation.</p>
<h3>Exposure triangle versus brightness in editing</h3>
<p>Increasing exposure or brightness in editing is not equivalent to capturing more light.</p>
<p>Post-production can remap recorded values, but it cannot restore fully clipped highlights or create clean shadow detail that was never captured. Raising a dark clip may reveal noise, compression artifacts, banding, or color shifts.</p>
<p>Good capture gives editing software better information to work with. <a href="/glossary/dynamic-range">Dynamic range</a>, bit depth, codec, and picture profile determine how far a file can be adjusted before it breaks down.</p>
<h3>Common exposure-triangle mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Believing ISO physically makes the sensor collect more light.</li>
<li>Changing shutter speed only to fix brightness and accidentally changing the motion look.</li>
<li>Recording every shot wide open and losing focus during subject movement.</li>
<li>Following the meter without checking important highlights or skin tones.</li>
<li>Using the lowest possible ISO while severely underexposing the image.</li>
<li>Forgetting that high frame rates require shorter exposures and usually more light.</li>
<li>Trying to solve a lighting problem entirely with camera settings.</li>
<li>Treating the 180-degree shutter guideline as an unbreakable rule.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Which exposure-triangle setting should I change first?</h4>
<p>For video, choose frame rate and shutter behavior first, then aperture for the desired depth of field. Control the actual lighting and use ISO to place the recorded signal appropriately. The order can change when motion, focus, or available light is the highest priority.</p>
<h4>Is ISO really part of exposure if it does not add light?</h4>
<p>Yes as a practical camera-control model, because ISO changes recorded brightness and influences the usable combination of shutter and aperture. Technically, it is different from controls that alter the amount of captured light.</p>
<h4>What does one stop mean?</h4>
<p>One stop is a doubling or halving of light or brightness. Moving from ISO 400 to 800 is one stop brighter in signal gain. Changing from 1/50 to 1/100 second captures one stop less light. Full aperture-stop sequences also change exposure by factors of two.</p>
<h4>Should the exposure meter always read zero?</h4>
<p>No. Zero reflects the camera's metering target, not necessarily the correct creative exposure. Check the subject, highlights, waveform, histogram, or zebras and expose for the intended result.</p>
