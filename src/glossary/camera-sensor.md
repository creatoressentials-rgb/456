<h2>Camera Sensor</h2>
<p>A <strong>camera sensor</strong> is the electronic component that receives light from the lens and converts it into image data.</p>
<p>It performs the role that film once performed in traditional cameras.</p>
<p>The sensor helps determine:</p>
<ul>
<li>Resolution</li>
<li>Field of view</li>
<li>Crop factor</li>
<li>Low-light performance</li>
<li>Dynamic range</li>
<li>Rolling shutter</li>
<li>Frame-rate capability</li>
<li>Autofocus design</li>
<li>Recording crop</li>
<li>Heat and data demands</li>
</ul>
<p>Sensor size is important, but it is only one part of camera performance.</p>
<h3>How a camera sensor works</h3>
<p>Light passes through the <a href="/glossary/camera-lens/">camera lens</a> and reaches millions of light-sensitive sites on the sensor.</p>
<p>The camera then:</p>
<ol>
<li>Measures incoming light</li>
<li>Converts it into electrical signals</li>
<li>Processes color and brightness</li>
<li>Creates video frames</li>
<li>Compresses and stores the footage</li>
</ol>

<h3>Sensor size and field of view</h3>
<p>A smaller sensor captures a smaller portion of the image projected by the lens.</p>
<p>This creates a narrower angle of view.</p>
<p><a href="/glossary/crop-factor/">Crop factor</a> helps compare that field of view with full frame.</p>
<h3>Sensor size and low light</h3>
<p>A larger sensor can collect more total light when comparing equivalent images under controlled conditions.</p>
<p>However, actual low-light performance also depends on:</p>
<ul>
<li>Sensor generation</li>
<li>Pixel design</li>
<li>Resolution</li>
<li>Exposure</li>
<li>Lens aperture</li>
<li>Processing</li>
<li>Noise reduction</li>
<li>Recording format</li>
</ul>
<p>A newer smaller-sensor camera can outperform an older larger-sensor camera.</p>
<h3>Sensor resolution</h3>
<p>Resolution describes the number of recorded pixels.</p>
<p>Higher resolution can provide:</p>
<ul>
<li>More detail</li>
<li>Cropping flexibility</li>
<li>Oversampled video</li>
<li>Larger still images</li>
</ul>
<p>It can also create:</p>
<ul>
<li>Larger files</li>
<li>More heat</li>
<li>Slower readout</li>
<li>Higher storage demands</li>
</ul>
<p>Megapixels alone do not determine video quality.</p>
<h3>Sensor readout</h3>
<p>The camera must read sensor data to create each frame.</p>
<p>A slow readout can produce:</p>
<ul>
<li>Rolling-shutter skew</li>
<li>Wobble</li>
<li>Bent vertical lines</li>
<li>Distorted fast movement</li>
</ul>
<p>A fast readout benefits handheld video, pans, sports, and moving subjects.</p>
<h3>CMOS sensors</h3>
<p>Most modern creator cameras use CMOS sensors.</p>
<p>Related designs include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Front-side illuminated</li>
<li>Back-side illuminated</li>
<li>Stacked CMOS</li>
<li>Partially stacked sensors</li>
</ul>
<p>These designs can affect speed, noise, cost, and power use.</p>
<h3>Sensor and dynamic range</h3>
<p><a href="/glossary/dynamic-range/">Dynamic range</a> is the span between bright and dark detail a camera can record.</p>
<p>Sensor design matters, but dynamic range also depends on:</p>
<ul>
<li>ISO</li>
<li>Picture profile</li>
<li>Bit depth</li>
<li>Codec</li>
<li>Exposure</li>
<li>Processing</li>
</ul>
<h3>Sensor and autofocus</h3>
<p>Some cameras include phase-detection focus points on the sensor.</p>
<p>Autofocus quality depends on the complete system:</p>
<ul>
<li>Sensor</li>
<li>Processor</li>
<li>Algorithm</li>
<li>Lens</li>
<li>Firmware</li>
<li>Subject detection</li>
</ul>
<p>Sensor size alone does not guarantee better <a href="/glossary/autofocus/">autofocus</a>.</p>
<h3>Recording crops</h3>
<p>A camera may use:</p>
<ul>
<li>Full sensor width</li>
<li>APS-C or Super 35 crop</li>
<li>Pixel-to-pixel crop</li>
<li>Electronic-stabilization crop</li>
<li>High-frame-rate crop</li>
</ul>
<p>The same camera can provide different fields of view in different recording modes.</p>
<h3>Choosing a sensor format for YouTube</h3>
<p>Prioritize the actual use case.</p>
<h4>APS-C</h4>
<p>Strong fit for affordability, compact lenses, travel, and solo recording.</p>
<h4>Full frame</h4>
<p>Useful for wide views, shallow depth of field, low-light potential, and hybrid commercial work.</p>
<h4>Smaller sensors</h4>
<p>Useful for portability, long recording, deep focus, action cameras, smartphones, and compact systems.</p>
<h3>Common sensor myths</h3>
<ul>
<li>More megapixels always means better video</li>
<li>Full frame is always professional</li>
<li>Smaller sensors cannot produce high-quality footage</li>
<li>Crop factor changes lens focal length</li>
<li>Larger sensors always autofocus better</li>
<li>Sensor size alone determines low-light quality</li>
<li>Every 4K mode uses the whole sensor</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>What does a camera sensor do?</h4>
<p>It converts light focused by the lens into electronic image data.</p>
<h4>Is a larger sensor always better?</h4>
<p>No. Size creates tradeoffs, while readout speed, processing, autofocus, lenses, cost, and recording modes also matter.</p>
<h4>Does sensor size affect focal length?</h4>
<p>It does not change the lens's focal length, but it changes the recorded field of view.</p>
<h4>How many megapixels does YouTube video need?</h4>
<p>Resolution requirements depend on recording format. A high-quality 4K image does not require the highest-megapixel still-photo sensor.</p>
