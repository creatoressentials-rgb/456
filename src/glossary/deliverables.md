<h2>Deliverables</h2>
<p><strong>Deliverables</strong> are the specific content assets, services, actions, or reports that a creator agrees to provide as part of a project or <a href="/glossary/brand-deal/">brand deal</a>. They turn a general campaign idea into a measurable scope of work.</p>
<p>Examples include one 60-second YouTube integration, one dedicated sponsored video, three Shorts, a thumbnail, a set of raw vertical clips, a livestream appearance, a link in the description, or a post-campaign analytics report.</p>
<p>The deliverables should be written into the contract or statement of work rather than left as informal assumptions in email. Creator-campaign systems commonly use a statement of work to connect compensation with the content types, platforms, tasks, rights, and deadlines assigned to a creator.</p>
<h3>What makes a deliverable specific?</h3>
<p>A well-written deliverable answers the questions needed to know whether the work has been completed:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>What is being made?</strong> Video, Short, post, thumbnail, script, raw footage, livestream segment, or report</li>
<li><strong>How many?</strong> Exact asset count or quantity</li>
<li><strong>Where will it appear?</strong> Creator channel, brand account, advertising library, event, or another platform</li>
<li><strong>What format is required?</strong> Orientation, resolution, file type, duration, language, or caption format</li>
<li><strong>What must it contain?</strong> Product demonstration, key message, call to action, link, code, disclosure, or required visual</li>
<li><strong>When is it due?</strong> Concept, draft, approval, publication, and reporting dates</li>
<li><strong>Who reviews it?</strong> Named decision maker or approval team</li>
<li><strong>How many revisions are included?</strong> Define the <a href="/glossary/revision-round/">revision rounds</a></li>
<li><strong>How long must it remain live?</strong> Permanent, minimum term, or specified end date</li>
<li><strong>What proof is required?</strong> Published URL, screenshots, source files, or analytics report</li>
</ul>
<p>“Make one YouTube video” is vague. “Create and publish one 8–12 minute 16:9 YouTube video containing a 60–90 second mid-video product integration, verbal and on-screen sponsorship disclosure, tracked description link, one consolidated revision round, and 30-day performance report” is much more actionable.</p>
<h3>Common creator deliverables</h3>
<h4>Published content</h4>
<ul>
<li>Long-form YouTube video</li>
<li>Sponsored integration inside a broader video</li>
<li>Dedicated sponsored video</li>
<li>YouTube Short</li>
<li>Livestream or sponsored live segment</li>
<li>Community post</li>
<li>Cross-platform Reel, TikTok, Story set, carousel, blog post, podcast mention, or newsletter placement</li>
</ul>
<h4>Production assets</h4>
<ul>
<li>Concepts or treatments</li>
<li>Script, talking points, or storyboard</li>
<li>Thumbnail or title options</li>
<li>Edited master file</li>
<li>Cutdowns in other aspect ratios</li>
<li>Captions or subtitle files</li>
<li>Raw footage, photography, or audio stems</li>
<li>Editable project files</li>
</ul>
<h4>Campaign actions</h4>
<ul>
<li>Attend an event or briefing</li>
<li>Join a product launch or livestream</li>
<li>Add a link, discount code, pinned comment, product tag, or call to action</li>
<li>Submit content for review</li>
<li>Keep content live for a minimum period</li>
<li>Grant brand partner access after the required agreement is in place</li>
</ul>
<h4>Reporting deliverables</h4>
<ul>
<li>Published URLs</li>
<li>Screenshots of platform metrics</li>
<li>Views, watch time, engagement, clicks, or conversions after a stated reporting period</li>
<li>Audience demographics when permitted</li>
<li>Campaign recap or creative learnings</li>
</ul>

<p>A campaign may measure views or sales, but those results are not automatically promised deliverables. Organic distribution is affected by audience behavior and platform systems. A creator should not guarantee a performance outcome unless the agreement intentionally creates that obligation and explains the remedy.</p>
<h3>How deliverables connect to the brief and contract</h3>
<p>The <a href="/glossary/campaign-brief/">campaign brief</a> explains the campaign objective, audience, message, and schedule. The <a href="/glossary/creative-brief/">creative brief</a> directs the concept, tone, claims, and execution.</p>
<p>The contract or statement of work should identify the binding deliverables, compensation, rights, deadlines, approval process, cancellation terms, and other business obligations. A brief may be incorporated into the agreement, but an informal brief alone can leave critical terms unresolved.</p>
<h3>How to write a YouTube integration deliverable</h3>
<p>A YouTube integration can specify:</p>
<ul>
<li>One long-form video on the creator’s named channel</li>
<li>Approximate total video length, when relevant</li>
<li>Sponsored-segment length range</li>
<li>Segment placement, such as before the midpoint or after a natural opening</li>
<li>Required product use or demonstration</li>
<li>Mandatory factual claims and prohibited claims</li>
<li>Verbal and visual disclosure</li>
<li>Call to action, tracked URL, coupon code, and description placement</li>
<li>Whether the brand may review the segment or the full video</li>
<li>Number of feedback rounds</li>
<li>Draft and publication dates</li>
<li>Minimum public availability period</li>
<li>Reporting date and metrics</li>
<li>Whether any usage, amplification, or exclusivity is included</li>
</ul>
<p>The creator should retain room to speak naturally and truthfully. A deliverable can require accurate product facts without forcing an opinion the creator does not hold.</p>
<h3>Acceptance and approval</h3>
<p>The agreement should explain what the brand is reviewing. Common approval stages include:</p>
<ol>
<li>Concept or outline</li>
<li>Script or talking points</li>
<li>Rough cut or sponsored segment</li>
<li>Final cut</li>
<li>Caption, title, thumbnail, link, and publication details</li>
</ol>
<p>Define who has authority to approve the work and how quickly feedback must be returned. Multiple stakeholders sending conflicting notes can create accidental extra rounds.</p>
<p>Acceptance criteria should be objective where possible. “Brand likes the video” is difficult to enforce; “video includes the approved disclosure, product demonstration, and call to action” is clearer.</p>
<h3>Deliverables and scope changes</h3>
<p>A requested change is more likely to be new scope when it adds an asset, changes the concept after approval, requires reshooting for a new message, changes the platform or format, expands rights, shortens the deadline, or introduces a new stakeholder’s requirements.</p>
<p>The creator and brand can handle new scope through a written change order that updates the fee, deliverables, schedule, and rights. This is more professional than arguing after the work has already been completed.</p>
<h3>Deliverables and usage rights</h3>
<p>Delivering a file does not automatically transfer ownership or unlimited usage rights. The agreement should separately state:</p>
<ul>
<li>Who owns the finished content and source materials</li>
<li>Whether the brand can repost it organically</li>
<li>Whether the brand can edit or create derivative versions</li>
<li>Whether it can be used in paid advertising</li>
<li>Which channels, territories, and media are allowed</li>
<li>The start date and duration of the permission</li>
<li>Whether the creator’s name, image, and likeness may be used</li>
</ul>
<p>On YouTube, brand partner access can allow performance sharing and promotion, but YouTube advises creators to handle usage-right agreements independently.</p>
<h3>Common deliverable mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Listing only the platform and quantity:</strong> The actual scope remains ambiguous.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming raw footage is included:</strong> Source files should be named separately.</li>
<li><strong>Leaving revision limits open-ended:</strong> Feedback can continue indefinitely.</li>
<li><strong>Using “perpetual” without understanding it:</strong> The brand may receive long-term value far beyond the original post.</li>
<li><strong>Confusing a KPI with a guarantee:</strong> Expected reach is not the same as promised delivery.</li>
<li><strong>Forgetting the live period:</strong> The parties may disagree about when content can be removed.</li>
<li><strong>Skipping reporting details:</strong> The creator may not know which metrics or reporting date are required.</li>
<li><strong>Changing the brief after production:</strong> Major changes may require a new fee and deadline.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Are deliverables the same as content?</h4>
<p>Not always. Content assets are common deliverables, but a deliverable can also be an appearance, report, link placement, event service, raw-file package, or another agreed action.</p>
<h4>Are usage rights a deliverable?</h4>
<p>They are usually better treated as separate commercial permissions attached to the deliverable. The asset describes what is made; the rights describe how the brand may use it.</p>
<h4>Is a view guarantee a deliverable?</h4>
<p>Only when the contract expressly makes it one. Most creator agreements require the creator to produce and publish content but do not guarantee organic views, sales, or engagement.</p>
<h4>What happens when a brand asks for another asset?</h4>
<p>If it was not included in the agreed scope, the parties can add it through a written change order with an additional fee and adjusted schedule.</p>
<h4>Should analytics reporting be listed as a deliverable?</h4>
<p>Yes, when the brand expects it. State which metrics will be provided, the reporting window, the format, and any platform-access limits.</p>
