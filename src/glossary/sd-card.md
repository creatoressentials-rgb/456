<h2>SD Card</h2>
<p>An <strong>SD card</strong> is a removable flash-memory card used to store photos, videos, audio, project files, and other data in cameras, recorders, drones, computers, and mobile devices.</p>
<p>SD cards have no moving mechanical parts. They are compact and rewritable, but capacity, physical size, speed, file system, and host compatibility vary across the SD family.</p>

<h3>Full-Size SD vs. microSD</h3>
<table><thead><tr><th>Form Factor</th><th>Common Uses</th><th>Important Note</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Full-size SD</td><td>Mirrorless cameras, DSLRs, camcorders, audio recorders</td><td>Includes a physical write-protect switch on the card shell</td></tr>
<tr><td>microSD</td><td>Action cameras, drones, phones, compact devices</td><td>Can fit some full-size slots through a passive adapter</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>An adapter changes the physical fit; it does not make the card faster. The device, card, adapter, reader, bus interface, and file system must all be compatible.</p>

<h3>SD, SDHC, SDXC, and SDUC</h3>
<table><thead><tr><th>Capacity Standard</th><th>Defined Capacity Range</th><th>Default File System</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>SD</td><td>Up to 2GB</td><td>FAT12 or FAT16</td></tr>
<tr><td>SDHC</td><td>More than 2GB through 32GB</td><td>FAT32</td></tr>
<tr><td>SDXC</td><td>More than 32GB through 2TB</td><td>exFAT</td></tr>
<tr><td>SDUC</td><td>More than 2TB through 128TB</td><td>exFAT</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>These labels describe capacity standards, not guaranteed speed. An SDXC card is not automatically faster than an SDHC card.</p>

<h3>What Do the Symbols on an SD Card Mean?</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Capacity label:</strong> SD, SDHC, SDXC, or SDUC.</li>
<li><strong>Bus interface:</strong> UHS-I, UHS-II, UHS-III, or SD Express indicates the communication interface.</li>
<li><strong>Speed class:</strong> C, U, V, or E symbols indicate defined minimum performance classes.</li>
<li><strong>Advertised read/write speed:</strong> A manufacturer may list maximum sequential speeds under specific test conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Application Performance Class:</strong> A1 or A2 relates to minimum random and sequential performance for running applications, primarily on supported mobile devices.</li>
</ul>

<h3>How Do You Choose an SD Card for Video?</h3>
<ol>
<li>Check the camera or recorder's official card-compatibility requirements.</li>
<li>Choose a supported capacity family and physical form factor.</li>
<li>Match the required sustained-write speed class to the recording mode.</li>
<li>Confirm whether the device supports UHS-I, UHS-II, or another bus interface.</li>
<li>Estimate capacity using the camera's bitrate, not resolution alone.</li>
<li>Buy from an authorized seller to reduce counterfeit risk.</li>
<li>Test the card in the actual device before an important shoot.</li>
</ol>

<h3>How Much Video Fits on an SD Card?</h3>
<p>Recording time depends primarily on bitrate. Two cameras can both record 4K while generating very different file sizes because they use different frame rates, codecs, bit depths, compression settings, and bitrates.</p>
<p>Use the camera's estimated recording-time display or calculate from its published bitrate. Leave spare capacity rather than planning to fill a card completely during a critical recording.</p>

<h3>How Should You Format an SD Card?</h3>
<p>Format the card in the camera or recording device when the manufacturer recommends it. This helps create the file structure expected by that device.</p>
<p>The SD Association also provides an SD Memory Card Formatter for cards that need standards-aligned formatting on a computer. Formatting normally erases access to existing files, so copy and verify needed footage first.</p>

<h3>SD Card Care and Workflow</h3>
<ul>
<li>Stop recording and wait for the activity light before removing the card.</li>
<li>Power down the device when its instructions require it.</li>
<li>Use a card case to protect contacts and prevent loss.</li>
<li>Label cards and establish clear empty, active, and backed-up states.</li>
<li>Copy footage to working storage, then verify the copy before formatting.</li>
<li>Keep more than one copy of important footage on separate storage devices.</li>
<li>Retire cards that produce repeated errors, corrupted files, or inconsistent performance.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Common SD Card Mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Assuming a large capacity label also guarantees enough recording speed.</li>
<li>Buying a fast card that the camera's slower slot cannot fully use.</li>
<li>Relying on a microSD adapter to improve performance.</li>
<li>Deleting individual files repeatedly instead of following the camera maker's recommended format workflow.</li>
<li>Removing a card while data is still being written.</li>
<li>Treating the original card as a permanent archive or the only copy.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently Asked Questions</h3>
<h4>Does SD stand for Secure Digital?</h4>
<p>Yes. SD is the Secure Digital memory-card standard maintained by the SD Association.</p>
<h4>Is SDXC faster than SDHC?</h4>
<p>Not necessarily. SDHC and SDXC describe capacity and file-system families. Speed depends on the card's performance class, bus interface, implementation, and host device.</p>
<h4>Can microSD be used in an SD slot?</h4>
<p>Often, with a compatible passive adapter. The camera must still support the card's capacity standard and performance.</p>
<h4>Is an SD card a backup?</h4>
<p>It is one storage copy. Important footage should exist in at least one additional verified location before the card is erased or reused.</p>
