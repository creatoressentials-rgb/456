<h2>Close-Up</h2>
<p>A <strong>close-up</strong> is a tightly framed camera shot that makes a face, object, action, or detail occupy most of the image. It reduces surrounding context so the audience concentrates on expression, texture, movement, or information that might be missed in a wider shot.</p>
<p>For a person, a standard close-up commonly emphasizes the head and shoulders. Close-ups can also feature hands, controls, food, product details, or any other subject that needs visual emphasis.</p>

<h3>What Does a Close-Up Show?</h3>
<ul>
<li>Facial expression and eye movement</li>
<li>Small actions that are difficult to read in a wider frame</li>
<li>Product buttons, ports, materials, or defects</li>
<li>Hands performing a precise task</li>
<li>Important story objects or visual evidence</li>
<li>Texture, emotion, reaction, and detail</li>
</ul>

<p>These boundaries are conventions rather than fixed measurements. Describe the intended crop in a <a href="/glossary/shot-list/">shot list</a> when exact framing matters.</p>

<h3>Close-Up vs. Insert Shot</h3>
<p>A close-up describes shot size. An insert describes editorial purpose: it isolates a specific object or action within the scene. Many insert shots are close-ups, but a close-up of a person's reaction is not automatically an insert.</p>

<h3>How YouTube Creators Use Close-Ups</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Talking-head videos:</strong> Emphasize an emotional statement or reaction.</li>
<li><strong>Product reviews:</strong> Show ports, screen details, materials, wear, and controls.</li>
<li><strong>Tutorials:</strong> Make hand placement and small steps visible.</li>
<li><strong>Food videos:</strong> Reveal texture, steam, slicing, and finished details.</li>
<li><strong>Unboxings:</strong> Highlight packaging, accessories, and product condition.</li>
<li><strong>Comedy:</strong> Hold on a reaction or reveal a visual joke.</li>
<li><strong>Documentaries:</strong> Show expressions, evidence, and meaningful objects.</li>
</ul>

<h3>How to Frame a Face in Close-Up</h3>
<ol>
<li>Place the eyes intentionally and preserve the expression as the main point of attention.</li>
<li>Decide whether the full head should remain visible or whether a tighter crop is deliberate.</li>
<li>Avoid an uncertain crop that barely clips hair, chin, or ears without a visual reason.</li>
<li>Focus carefully on the nearest or most important eye.</li>
<li>Check skin highlights, glasses reflections, and background distractions.</li>
<li>Leave look room when the subject faces away from the lens.</li>
<li>Use a camera distance that avoids unwanted facial perspective distortion.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Does a Close-Up Require a Telephoto Lens?</h3>
<p>No. Close-up describes the resulting framing, not a required focal length. A longer lens can create tight framing from farther away, while a wider lens can create a close-up from a short distance.</p>
<p>However, moving very close with a wide lens can exaggerate the size of the nose, hands, or nearest product feature. Camera distance changes perspective, so choose both distance and focal length deliberately.</p>

<h3>Focus and Depth of Field</h3>
<p>Close-ups make focus errors more visible. A shallow depth of field can isolate an eye or product detail, but it can also make natural movement fall out of focus.</p>
<ul>
<li>Use reliable eye or subject autofocus when appropriate.</li>
<li>Stop down the aperture when more of the face or object must remain sharp.</li>
<li>Increase light rather than accepting an unusably narrow focus zone.</li>
<li>Test autofocus transitions before recording demonstrations.</li>
<li>Use manual focus or focus marks for repeatable product shots.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Close-Ups in Editing</h3>
<p>Close-ups can reveal a reaction, clarify an action, hide a continuity edit, or change the emotional intensity of a sequence. They work best when the wider context has been established or when the detail itself provides enough information.</p>
<p>Using only close-ups can make viewers lose track of space and movement. Combine them with <a href="/glossary/wide-shot/">wide shots</a> and medium coverage when orientation matters.</p>

<h3>Common Close-Up Mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Equating close-up with one specific lens.</li>
<li>Focusing on eyelashes, glasses, or a nearby object instead of the eye.</li>
<li>Using such shallow depth of field that normal movement ruins focus.</li>
<li>Cropping accidentally rather than making a confident tight composition.</li>
<li>Showing a detail before viewers understand where it is located.</li>
<li>Using a distant microphone because the image appears visually close.</li>
<li>Overusing close-ups until the scene loses spatial context.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently Asked Questions</h3>
<h4>What is a standard close-up of a person?</h4>
<p>It commonly frames the head and shoulders with the face occupying much of the image, although exact boundaries vary.</p>
<h4>Is a close-up the same as a zoomed-in shot?</h4>
<p>Not necessarily. A close-up can be created through camera position, lens choice, optical zoom, or cropping. Each method can produce different perspective and quality.</p>
<h4>Can a close-up crop the top of the head?</h4>
<p>Yes. Tight close-ups often crop part of the hair or head deliberately, provided the eyes and expression remain well composed.</p>
<h4>Why use a close-up after a wide shot?</h4>
<p>The wide shot establishes location and relationships; the close-up then directs attention to a specific expression, action, or detail.</p>
