<h2>Reused Content</h2>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> Reused content is YouTube's monetization policy covering videos built from someone else's material — reaction videos, clips, compilations, remixes. It's fine to monetize if you add clear original commentary, editing, or value; it's a violation if your upload is basically the source material with little added on top. This is a policy about <em>derivative</em> content — don't confuse it with Inauthentic Content, YouTube's separate channel-wide policy about mass-produced or templated uploads. See the full breakdown in our YouTube Monetization &amp; Policies hub.</p>

<h3>What counts as reused content</h3>
<p>YouTube defines it as content that repurposes material already on YouTube or elsewhere online <strong>without significant original commentary, substantive modifications, or educational/entertainment value.</strong> It also covers scraped or duplicative content — taking someone else's work from another site and posting it as your own.</p>
<p>The test YouTube itself applies: <strong>can a viewer tell there's a meaningful difference between the original video and yours?</strong> If yes, you've transformed it enough. If your video and the source are basically interchangeable, it's a violation — even if you had the original creator's permission.</p>
<p><strong>Important:</strong> this is separate from copyright. You can have full permission from the original creator, or clear every Content ID claim, and still violate the Reused Content policy — because it isn't based on copyright, fair use, or consent. It's based on whether <em>you</em> visibly made something new. Conversely, clearing this policy doesn't exempt you from <a href="/glossary/copyright-claim/">Copyright Claims</a> or <a href="/glossary/content-id/">Content ID</a> — those are handled independently.</p>

<h3>The "added value" bar, in practice</h3>
<p>YouTube reviewers look at how you created, participated in, or produced the content — checking your videos, channel description, and titles/descriptions to judge intent, not just running a single clip through a checklist. Enforcement applies to your <strong>whole channel</strong>: if reviewers can't clearly tell the content is yours, monetization can be pulled channel-wide, not just from the flagged video.</p>

<h4>Compliant (monetizable)</h4>
<ul>
<li>Clips used for a critical review or analysis</li>
<li>A movie/show scene with rewritten dialogue or a new voiceover</li>
<li>Sports replays where you break down the competitor's technique</li>
<li>Reaction videos where you're actively commenting on what's happening</li>
<li>Edited footage from other creators woven into your own storyline and commentary</li>
<li>Remixed Shorts — original content layered onto a licensed song, or your own audio/video combined with another clip</li>
<li>Content where you're the primary visible presence</li>
<li>Footage with your own audio/visual effects that clearly show substantive editing</li>
</ul>

<h4>Non-compliant (demonetized)</h4>
<ul>
<li>Clips strung together with little or no narrative</li>
<li>Short compilations pulled straight from other social platforms</li>
<li>Playlists/collections of other artists' songs, even with permission</li>
<li>Re-uploading content that's already been posted by others many times</li>
<li>Promoting someone else's content, even with their permission</li>
<li>Downloaded or copied content with no substantive changes</li>
<li>Reaction videos that are mostly non-verbal reactions with no added voice commentary — this one catches a lot of reaction channels off guard</li>
<li>Videos that just read text from a website or news feed aloud</li>
<li>A song with only the pitch or speed altered</li>
</ul>

<h3>Reused Content vs. Inauthentic Content</h3>
<p>These two policies get confused constantly because both can affect reaction/compilation-style channels. They are not the same thing.</p>

<p>A channel can violate one without touching the other. A reaction channel with genuine commentary is fine on both counts. An AI-narrated compilation channel with zero original insight can trip both at once — reused because it's someone else's footage, inauthentic because the format is templated and mass-produced.</p>

<h3>Practical guidance for reaction and commentary creators</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Talk. Constantly.</strong> YouTube specifically calls out "mostly non-verbal reactions... without added voice commentary" as non-compliant. Face-pulling and silence isn't enough — verbalize your reaction, analysis, and opinion.</li>
<li><strong>Don't rely on runtime ratio alone</strong>, but as a gut-check, if someone could mute your commentary track and lose nothing, you likely haven't added enough.</li>
<li><strong>Editorialize, don't just react.</strong> Explain why something matters, add context the original doesn't have, or build a throughline across the clips you're using.</li>
<li><strong>Permission isn't a shield.</strong> Licensing a clip or getting a shoutout from the original creator doesn't clear this policy — it only affects copyright risk, which is separate.</li>
<li><strong>Watermarks and un-edited re-uploads are the fastest way to get flagged.</strong> If your video and the source are visually near-identical, add visible original elements — not just a logo bug.</li>
<li><strong>Disclose AI use.</strong> If you use AI for voiceover, dubbing, or visuals in your reaction/commentary content, YouTube expects you to disclose altered or synthetic elements to your audience.</li>
<li><strong>If you're suspended:</strong> you can appeal within 21 days (decision in ~14 days), or reapply to the YouTube Partner Program 90 days after suspension once you've fixed or removed the violating content.</li>
</ul>

<h3>FAQ</h3>
<h4>Are reaction videos banned from monetization?</h4>
<p>No. YouTube explicitly reaffirmed in its July 15, 2025 policy update that reaction videos, clips, commentary, and compilations remain monetizable under the Reused Content policy — as long as you add real commentary and value, not just a reaction shot with no voice.</p>
<h4>Does having permission from the original creator protect me?</h4>
<p>Not from this policy. Reused Content enforcement is separate from copyright — permission affects copyright/Content ID risk, not whether reviewers consider your video "yours." You still need visible, substantive original contribution.</p>
<h4>If one video gets flagged, does it affect my whole channel?</h4>
<p>Yes, potentially. YouTube reviews the pattern across your channel, and if reviewers can't clearly tell you're the one making the content, monetization can be removed from the entire channel — not just the flagged video.</p>
