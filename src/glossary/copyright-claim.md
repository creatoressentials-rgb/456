<h2>Copyright Claim</h2>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> A copyright claim happens when YouTube's <a href="/glossary/content-id/">Content ID</a> system — or a manual review by a rights holder — matches your video against copyrighted material (usually music). The rights holder can then monetize, track, or block that one video. It's a video-level flag, not a channel-level penalty: a claim alone does not hurt your channel standing, your YouTube Monetization and Policies eligibility, or your search visibility, and it cannot get your channel terminated. That's a <a href="/glossary/copyright-strike/">copyright strike</a> — a different, more serious thing. See below for exactly how they differ.</p>

<h3>What a claim actually restricts</h3>
<p>Once Content ID finds a match, the rights holder chooses one of three outcomes for your video (and it can vary by country):</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Monetize</strong> — ads run on your video, and the ad revenue goes to the rights holder instead of you. This is the most common outcome. See <a href="/glossary/ad-revenue/">Ad Revenue</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Track</strong> — your video stays exactly as it is and stays fully viewable; the rights holder just gets to see its performance stats.</li>
<li><strong>Block</strong> — your video is taken down or blocked from view, sometimes only in specific regions.</li>
</ul>
<p>The claim itself doesn't touch your watch time, your <a href="/glossary/engaged-views/">engaged views</a>, or your channel's overall good standing. If the video is blocked in some countries, you'll naturally see fewer <a href="/glossary/unique-viewers/">unique viewers</a> and less <a href="/glossary/monetized-playbacks/">monetized playbacks</a> from those regions — but that's a side effect of the block, not a separate penalty.</p>
<p>In YouTube Studio, a claimed video shows a "Copyright" status under Content &gt; Restrictions. Click it for the specific claimant, the copyrighted work detected, and the exact timestamp.</p>

<h3>What you can do about it</h3>
<p>You generally have three options:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Dispute it</strong> — if you have a license, permission, or a genuine fair-use basis, or you believe Content ID made a mistake. Do this in Studio: Content → find the video → Restrictions column → Copyright → See details → Take action → Dispute.</li>
<li><strong>Remove or replace the claimed content</strong> — mute the flagged audio, swap it for a track from YouTube's Audio Library, or trim out the claimed segment. This often clears the claim without involving the rights holder at all, and is usually the fastest fix when you don't have a real dispute basis.</li>
<li><strong>Do nothing</strong> — leave the claim in place. The video stays up, revenue redirects to the rights holder, and your channel is otherwise unaffected. For a single claimed clip in an otherwise strong video, this is often the simplest, lowest-risk choice.</li>
</ol>

<h4>The dispute process and its real risk</h4>
<p>After you submit a dispute, the claimant has <strong>30 days</strong> to respond. They can release the claim (your monetization is restored, including revenue earned during the dispute window), reinstate it (your dispute is rejected, and you may be eligible to appeal), or let it expire automatically if they don't respond. If your video was blocked rather than monetized, you can skip ahead to "Escalate to Appeal," which cuts the claimant's response window to 7 days.</p>
<p><strong>Don't dispute reflexively.</strong> YouTube explicitly says these are not valid reasons to dispute: crediting the creator, owning a copy of the song, or simply choosing not to monetize. Disputing without a real license or fair-use basis carries genuine downside — if the claimant disagrees, they can escalate to a formal copyright removal request, which turns your claim into an actual strike. And YouTube warns that repeated or malicious dispute abuse can bring penalties on its own. If you're not sure you have the rights, muting or replacing the audio is almost always the safer move.</p>

<h3>Copyright Claim vs. Copyright Strike</h3>
<p>This is the distinction that trips up the most creators — and where a lot of other guides get sloppy.</p>

<tr><td>Triggered by</td><td>Content ID automated match, or a claimant's manual review</td><td>A legal takedown notice (DMCA) filed by the rights holder</td></tr>

<h3>FAQ</h3>
<h4>Does a copyright claim hurt my channel's standing or monetization eligibility?</h4>
<p>No. A claim applies to the individual video only. Your channel stays in good standing and your Partner Program eligibility is unaffected, even with multiple claims across different videos.</p>
<h4>Will a copyright claim go away on its own?</h4>
<p>Only if you resolve it — dispute it successfully, remove/replace the claimed content, or the claimant releases it. If you dispute and the claimant doesn't respond within 30 days, the claim expires automatically. Left alone otherwise, a monetize/track claim can persist indefinitely.</p>
<h4>Can I still earn money on a video with a claim on it?</h4>
<p>The video can still run ads, but if the rights holder chose to monetize the claim, that revenue goes to them, not you. Successfully disputing the claim restores your monetization, including revenue from the dispute period.</p>
