<h2>Wide-Angle Lens</h2>
<p>A <strong>wide-angle lens</strong> is a camera lens that captures a broad view of the scene.</p>
<p>For YouTubers, it is especially useful when:</p>
<ul>
<li>Filming in a small room</li>
<li>Holding the camera at arm's length</li>
<li>Showing a desk or studio</li>
<li>Recording travel and location videos</li>
<li>Filming groups</li>
<li>Creating real-estate or room-tour content</li>
<li>Showing both a subject and the surrounding environment</li>
</ul>
<p>A lens is considered wide because of the angle of view produced by the lens-and-camera combination—not simply because its focal-length number is low.</p>
<h3>What counts as a wide-angle lens?</h3>
<p>The exact range depends on sensor size.</p>
<p>Approximate full-frame-equivalent categories are:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
  <th>Category</th>
  <th style="text-align:right">Approximate equivalent focal length</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
  <td>Ultra-wide</td>
  <td style="text-align:right">Below about 20mm</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Wide angle</td>
  <td style="text-align:right">About 20–35mm</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Standard</td>
  <td style="text-align:right">About 35–70mm</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Telephoto</td>
  <td style="text-align:right">Above about 70mm</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>These boundaries are guidelines rather than official rules.</p>
<p>A 16mm lens is very wide on a full-frame camera. On a 1.5× APS-C camera, it produces a field of view similar to about 24mm on full frame.</p>

<p>The same actual lens produces different framing on different sensor sizes.</p>

<p>The focal length printed on the lens does not change. The smaller sensor captures a smaller part of the projected image.</p>
<p>See <a href="/glossary/crop-factor/">crop factor</a>.</p>
<h3>Why YouTubers use wide-angle lenses</h3>
<h4>Vlogging</h4>
<p>A wide lens can include the creator's face and surroundings when the camera is held close.</p>
<p>This is helpful for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Handheld travel videos</li>
<li>Walking videos</li>
<li>Behind-the-scenes content</li>
<li>Daily vlogs</li>
<li>Smartphone-style creator videos</li>
</ul>
<p>A lens can become effectively less wide when electronic stabilization crops the frame.</p>
<h4>Small-room filming</h4>
<p>A creator may not be able to move the camera farther from the desk or subject.</p>
<p>A wide lens allows more of the room to fit in the shot.</p>
<h4>Environmental storytelling</h4>
<p>A wide frame shows where the creator is—not just who the creator is.</p>
<p>This works well for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Workshops</li>
<li>Kitchens</li>
<li>Studios</li>
<li>Travel locations</li>
<li>Events</li>
<li>Retail spaces</li>
</ul>
<h4>Overhead and demonstration videos</h4>
<p>A wide lens can cover a larger table or workspace when the camera cannot be mounted very high.</p>

<h3>Does a wide lens distort faces?</h3>
<p>A wide lens can make facial features look exaggerated when the camera is placed very close.</p>
<p>For example:</p>
<ol>
<li>The camera is placed close to the creator's face.</li>
<li>The nose is much closer to the camera than the ears.</li>
<li>The nose appears proportionally larger.</li>
<li>The background appears farther away and smaller.</li>
</ol>
<p>The close camera position creates the perspective effect.</p>
<p>The wide lens makes it possible to remain close while still fitting the face into the frame.</p>
<p>To reduce exaggeration:</p>
<ul>
<li>Move the camera farther away</li>
<li>Use a longer focal length</li>
<li>Crop slightly</li>
<li>Keep the face away from extreme frame edges</li>
<li>Avoid placing the lens below or above the face unnecessarily</li>
</ul>
<h3>Edge stretching</h3>
<p>Wide lenses can stretch objects near the edges of the frame.</p>
<p>This is especially visible with:</p>
<ul>
<li>Faces near corners</li>
<li>Hands extended toward the lens</li>
<li>Straight architectural lines</li>
<li>Group members at the frame edge</li>
</ul>
<p>Keep important faces closer to the center when possible.</p>
<h3>Wide-angle distortion types</h3>
<h4>Perspective exaggeration</h4>
<p>Caused primarily by close camera position.</p>
<h4>Barrel distortion</h4>
<p>Straight lines bow outward because of lens design.</p>
<p>Camera or editing software may correct this automatically.</p>
<h4>Edge stretching</h4>
<p>Subjects near the edge appear wider or elongated.</p>
<h4>Keystoning</h4>
<p>Vertical lines lean inward or outward when the camera is tilted up or down.</p>
<p>This is common in room and architecture videos.</p>
<h3>Wide-angle lenses and depth of field</h3>
<p>Wide lenses often make it easier to keep more of the scene acceptably sharp.</p>
<p>However, depth of field also depends on:</p>
<ul>
<li><a href="/glossary/aperture/">Aperture</a></li>
<li>Sensor size</li>
<li>Focus distance</li>
<li>Subject distance</li>
<li>Background distance</li>
</ul>
<p>A wide f/1.4 lens focused close can still create substantial background blur.</p>
<h3>Wide-angle lenses and camera movement</h3>
<p>A wide lens often makes ordinary handheld shake less magnified than a telephoto lens.</p>
<p>However, movement near the frame edges can appear fast or stretched.</p>
<p>For walking videos, creators should consider:</p>
<ul>
<li>Optical stabilization</li>
<li>In-body stabilization</li>
<li>Electronic stabilization crop</li>
<li>Gimbal use</li>
<li>Rolling-shutter behavior</li>
<li>Horizon leveling</li>
</ul>
<h3>Choosing a wide lens for YouTube</h3>
<p>Check:</p>
<ul>
<li>Camera sensor size</li>
<li>Equivalent focal length</li>
<li>Room width</li>
<li>Camera-to-subject distance</li>
<li>Arm's-length framing</li>
<li>Stabilization crop</li>
<li>Maximum aperture</li>
<li>Autofocus noise</li>
<li>Minimum focusing distance</li>
<li>Lens weight</li>
<li>Filter compatibility</li>
<li>Distortion correction</li>
</ul>
<h3>Practical YouTube examples</h3>
<h4>Handheld vlog</h4>
<p>A full-frame-equivalent view around 16–24mm is commonly useful.</p>
<h4>Small-room talking head</h4>
<p>A full-frame-equivalent view around 24–35mm often balances room coverage with natural facial proportions, provided the camera is not too close.</p>
<h4>Real-estate walkthrough</h4>
<p>An ultra-wide lens can show rooms, but excessive width can misrepresent space.</p>
<h4>Product demonstration</h4>
<p>A moderately wide lens can include the creator and workspace, while a very wide lens may make the product look unnaturally large when held near the camera.</p>
<h3>Common mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Ignoring sensor size</li>
<li>Buying a lens that becomes too narrow after stabilization crop</li>
<li>Placing the camera too close to the face</li>
<li>Putting people at extreme frame edges</li>
<li>Assuming every low millimeter number is equally wide on every camera</li>
<li>Using an ultra-wide lens when a standard wide lens would look more natural</li>
<li>Tilting the camera and creating leaning walls</li>
<li>Ignoring autofocus and stabilization performance</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>What focal length is considered wide angle?</h4>
<p>On full frame, approximately 20–35mm is commonly called wide angle, with shorter focal lengths considered ultra-wide. Sensor size changes the resulting view.</p>
<h4>Is a wide-angle lens good for YouTube?</h4>
<p>Yes, especially for vlogging, small rooms, travel, groups, and environmental videos.</p>
<h4>Why does my face look strange with a wide lens?</h4>
<p>The camera is probably too close. Move it farther away and use a longer focal length or crop to preserve the framing.</p>
<h4>Is a wide-angle lens the same as a fisheye lens?</h4>
<p>No. A fisheye intentionally creates extreme curved distortion. Many wide-angle lenses are designed to keep straight lines comparatively straight.</p>
