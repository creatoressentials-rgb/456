<h2>L-Cut</h2>
<p>An <strong>L-cut</strong> is a video-editing transition in which audio from the current shot continues after the picture has changed to the next shot. The viewer sees the new image while still hearing dialogue, narration, ambience, music, or another sound from the previous one.</p>
<p>L-cuts are a type of <strong>split edit</strong> because the audio and video edit points occur at different times. They help creators connect shots smoothly, preserve a speaker's thought over supporting footage, show reactions while someone continues talking, and avoid the mechanical feeling of changing sound and picture at exactly the same frame.</p>
<p>The name comes from the approximate L shape created by staggered audio and video clips on a traditional editing <a href="/glossary/timeline/">timeline</a>. The outgoing audio extends farther to the right than its matching picture.</p>
<h3>How an L-cut works</h3>
<p>Imagine a creator finishing an explanation on camera. The video cuts to a close-up of the product, but the creator's sentence continues over the demonstration. The picture has moved forward while the previous audio carries the idea into the new shot.</p>
<p>A basic L-cut follows this sequence:</p>
<ol>
<li>The current shot and its audio play together.</li>
<li>The picture cuts to the next shot.</li>
<li>Audio from the previous shot continues beneath the new picture.</li>
<li>The outgoing audio ends or transitions into the next sound.</li>
</ol>
<p>The overlap may last only a few frames or several seconds. Its length should follow the meaning and rhythm of the audio rather than a fixed rule.</p>

<p>An L-cut carries the audience out of the previous shot. A J-cut pulls the audience toward the upcoming shot. Editors often use both techniques in the same sequence to make conversations, interviews, and narration feel less rigid.</p>
<h3>Why creators use L-cuts</h3>
<h4>To cover A-roll with supporting footage</h4>
<p>A creator can continue speaking while the picture changes from <a href="/glossary/a-roll/">A-roll</a> to a product close-up, screen recording, demonstration, location shot, chart, photograph, or other supporting visual. The audience keeps receiving the explanation without staring at the same talking-head frame.</p>
<p>This is one of the most common uses in tutorials, reviews, documentaries, essays, and educational videos.</p>
<h4>To show reactions during interviews</h4>
<p>The audience does not always need to watch the person who is speaking. An L-cut can show the listener, interviewer, customer, or group reacting while the previous speaker finishes the thought.</p>
<p>Reaction shots add emotion and context, but the outgoing audio must remain clear enough that the reaction does not distract from the message.</p>
<h4>To create smoother scene changes</h4>
<p>Natural sound can continue briefly across a location change. A crowd cheer, engine, kitchen sound, or room ambience can bridge the visual cut and prevent the transition from feeling empty or abrupt.</p>
<p>An L-cut can also let music carry through a montage while the images change independently.</p>
<h4>To preserve sentence rhythm</h4>
<p>Cutting away at a natural visual moment may be better than waiting for the exact end of a sentence. The speaker's audio can continue while the editor selects the strongest picture timing.</p>
<p>This separates visual pacing from speech pacing without changing the spoken performance.</p>
<h4>To hide edits and production limitations</h4>
<p>Supporting footage can cover a cut caused by a mistake, pause, camera adjustment, or shortened answer. The L-cut preserves continuous audio while the picture changes, making the repair less obvious than a visible <a href="/glossary/jump-cut/">jump cut</a>.</p>
<h3>How to create an L-cut</h3>
<p>The controls differ among editing applications, but the underlying method is consistent:</p>
<ol>
<li>Place the outgoing and incoming shots next to each other.</li>
<li>Separate, unlink, or independently trim the audio and video edit points.</li>
<li>Cut to the incoming picture before the outgoing audio has ended.</li>
<li>Extend or preserve the outgoing audio beneath the new shot.</li>
<li>Trim competing sound from the incoming clip when necessary.</li>
<li>Add a short fade only when it improves the transition.</li>
<li>Listen without watching the timeline to judge whether the overlap feels natural.</li>
</ol>
<p>Editors may use linked-selection controls, rolling edits, ripple edits, or dedicated split-edit commands. The technique works in Premiere Pro, Final Cut Pro, DaVinci Resolve, CapCut, and other editors; it is not tied to one application.</p>
<h3>Does an L-cut require a crossfade?</h3>
<p>No. An L-cut describes when outgoing audio ends relative to the picture cut. The sound can stop cleanly, fade out, blend into incoming room tone, or transition into another effect.</p>
<p>A crossfade may smooth two compatible ambience tracks, but it can also create doubled dialogue, phase problems, or a muddy transition. Use one only when the sound benefits from blending.</p>
<h3>L-cuts for common creator formats</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Talking-head videos:</strong> Keep narration playing while cutting to examples or graphics.</li>
<li><strong>Interviews:</strong> Show the listener's reaction before the speaker finishes.</li>
<li><strong>Tutorials:</strong> Continue instructions over a close-up or screen capture.</li>
<li><strong>Reviews:</strong> Carry commentary over product details, tests, or comparison shots.</li>
<li><strong>Travel videos:</strong> Let narration from one location continue over the first image of the next sequence.</li>
<li><strong>Gaming videos:</strong> Preserve commentary while changing gameplay views or inserting a replay.</li>
<li><strong>Documentaries:</strong> Continue interview audio over archival images or location footage.</li>
<li><strong>Vlogs:</strong> Bridge moments with continuous ambience or voiceover.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Common L-cut mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Letting two speakers compete:</strong> The outgoing line overlaps incoming dialogue and becomes difficult to understand.</li>
<li><strong>Using unrelated supporting footage:</strong> The new image pulls attention away from the continuing statement.</li>
<li><strong>Carrying audio too long:</strong> The audience becomes confused about which scene or person the sound belongs to.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring room-tone changes:</strong> A sudden noise-floor or ambience shift reveals the edit.</li>
<li><strong>Adding unnecessary crossfades:</strong> Overprocessing creates smeared words or unnatural background sound.</li>
<li><strong>Using every transition the same way:</strong> Repeated L-cuts can make pacing predictable.</li>
<li><strong>Confusing an L-cut with a J-cut:</strong> An L-cut carries previous audio forward; a J-cut begins upcoming audio early.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Why is it called an L-cut?</h4>
<p>When linked audio and video clips are trimmed to different end points, their edges can form an approximate L shape on a layered timeline.</p>
<h4>What is the difference between an L-cut and a J-cut?</h4>
<p>An L-cut lets the previous shot's audio continue after the next picture appears. A J-cut starts the next shot's audio before its picture appears.</p>
<h4>Can an L-cut use music or ambience?</h4>
<p>Yes. The outgoing sound can be dialogue, narration, music, ambience, or an effect. The technique is defined by timing rather than sound type.</p>
<h4>How long should an L-cut last?</h4>
<p>There is no standard duration. Keep the overlap long enough to preserve meaning or flow, but short enough that the audience understands the relationship between the sound and image.</p>
<h4>Is an L-cut the same as <a href="/glossary/b-roll/">B-roll</a>?</h4>
<p>No. An L-cut is an audio-video timing technique. Supporting footage is one type of image that may appear while the previous audio continues.</p>
