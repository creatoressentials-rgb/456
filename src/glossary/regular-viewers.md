<h2>Regular Viewers</h2>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> Regular viewers are the people in your YouTube Analytics "Audience" tab who watched at least one of your videos, at least once a month, for <strong>more than 6 months out of the past 12</strong> — YouTube's own definition of your most loyal audience segment. It's a deliberately high bar, so don't panic if the number looks small next to your subscriber count.</p>

<h3>The exact definition and threshold</h3>
<p>YouTube's monthly audience (<a href="/glossary/unique-viewers/">unique viewers</a> in the trailing 28 days) gets split into three tiers based on watch history over the past 12 months:</p>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Segment</th><th>Definition</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td><strong>New viewers</strong></td><td>Watched your channel for the first time in the selected period. (Private-browsing sessions, deleted watch history, or a 12+ month gap also count as "new.")</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Casual viewers</strong></td><td>Watched at least once a month for <strong>1–5 months</strong> of the past year.</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Regular viewers</strong></td><td>Watched at least once a month for <strong>more than 6 months</strong> of the past year — 7 or more of the trailing 12 months.</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>This system replaced YouTube's older, binary "<a href="/glossary/returning-viewers/">returning viewers</a>" metric in mid-2025. If you're used to that term, note that it no longer exists in Analytics — it's been split into Casual and Regular so you can see <em>how</em> loyal your returning audience actually is, not just that they returned once.</p>

<h3>Regular Viewers vs. Casual Viewers vs. Returning Viewers vs. Subscribers</h3>
<p>These four terms get mixed up constantly. Here's the distinction:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Casual viewers</strong> watched you sporadically (1–5 months out of 12). They know your channel but haven't built a habit.</li>
<li><strong>Regular viewers</strong> watched you consistently (7+ months out of 12). This is your core community.</li>
<li><strong>Returning viewers</strong> is the deprecated metric these two replaced — if you see it referenced in an old guide, mentally translate it to "casual + regular."</li>
<li><strong>Subscribers</strong> just clicked a button. Subscribing doesn't require ever watching a video again — YouTube's own data shows subscribers watch roughly 2x as much as non-subscribers on average, but plenty of subscribers go quiet. Your regular viewer count is almost always smaller than your subscriber count, and that's normal, not a red flag.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Why your regular viewer number looks low</h3>
<p>A regular viewer share under 1% is common, especially if your channel is newer, trend-driven, or Shorts-heavy — Shorts pull in a lot of discovery-based, one-off viewing that rarely clears a 7-month bar. Series-based, topic-focused channels with a consistent host tend to post noticeably higher regular viewer shares because the format itself rewards repeat monthly viewing. Read your percentage in the context of <em>your</em> content type, not as an absolute score — and remember YouTube has confirmed this metric doesn't affect reach, recommendations, or monetization eligibility either way.</p>

<h3>How to find it</h3>
<p><a href="/glossary/youtube-studio/">YouTube Studio</a> → <strong>Analytics</strong> → <strong>Audience</strong> tab → the "New, casual, and regular viewers" card under your monthly audience chart. Data refreshes every 1–2 days, and you can view it across 7-, 28-, or 90-day windows.</p>

<h3>How to grow regular viewers</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Give viewers a reason to come back monthly, not just once.</strong> Series, recurring segments, or a consistent weekly upload slot all target the "once a month for 6+ months" window directly — sporadic uploads make it mathematically harder for anyone to qualify.</li>
<li><strong>Keep format and topic narrow enough to be recognizable.</strong> A channel that jumps between unrelated topics attracts new/casual viewers but struggles to convert them, since viewers need a reason to specifically seek you out again.</li>
<li><strong>Use community posts and pinned comments between uploads.</strong> These keep your channel visible in feeds during gaps, which matters for the monthly cadence this metric tracks.</li>
<li><strong>Reply to comments and run live premieres/chats.</strong> Direct interaction is the single most-repeated tactic across creator guidance on this metric — it converts casual, one-off watchers into people who check back.</li>
<li><strong>Keep branding and hosting consistent.</strong> Familiar intros, <a href="/glossary/youtube-thumbnails/">thumbnails</a>, and on-screen talent reduce the "is this the same channel?" friction that quietly kills repeat viewership.</li>
<li><strong>Track the trend, not the snapshot.</strong> Because this is a trailing 12-month calculation, a single great video won't move it much — watch whether your regular viewer <em>percentage</em> rises over multiple months as a sign your <a href="/glossary/retention/">retention</a> strategy is working.</li>
</ul>

<h3>FAQ</h3>
<h4>Does a low regular viewer count hurt my monetization or reach?</h4>
<p>No. YouTube has stated explicitly that viewer segment data doesn't affect recommendations, reach, or Partner Program eligibility. It's a diagnostic metric, not a ranking factor.</p>
<h4>Is "regular viewers" the same as "returning viewers"?</h4>
<p>No — "returning viewers" was YouTube's old, binary metric (watched before vs. hasn't) and has been retired. Regular viewers is the stricter of its two replacements, requiring 7+ months of monthly return visits in the past year; casual viewers is the looser one (1–5 months).</p>
<h4>Why is my regular viewer percentage so much lower than my subscriber count?</h4>
<p>Because subscribing takes one click and never expires, while regular viewer status requires actually watching monthly for over half the year. Most channels have far more inactive subscribers than active regular viewers — this gap is expected, not a sign of a problem.</p>
