<h2>Overhead Camera Mount</h2>
<p>An <strong>overhead camera mount</strong> is a support system that positions a camera or phone above a subject, usually with the lens aimed downward. It is used to create top-down views of desks, tables, workbenches, floors, food, products, documents, crafts, drawings, repairs, games, and demonstrations.</p>
<p>The term describes the mounting equipment, not the camera angle itself. An overhead shot is the resulting view; the mount is the rig that holds the camera in that position.</p>

<h3>What Is an Overhead Camera Mount Used For?</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Unboxing and product videos:</strong> Show products and hands clearly on a tabletop.</li>
  <li><strong>Cooking videos:</strong> Record ingredients, cutting, mixing, and plating from above.</li>
  <li><strong>Art and craft tutorials:</strong> Keep drawings, tools, and hand movements visible.</li>
  <li><strong>Board-game content:</strong> Capture the complete play area with repeatable framing.</li>
  <li><strong>Repair and assembly videos:</strong> Provide a direct view of small parts and procedures.</li>
  <li><strong>Document and book capture:</strong> Hold the camera parallel to a flat subject.</li>
  <li><strong>Product photography:</strong> Create flat lays and consistent catalog-style images.</li>
</ul>

<p>A standard <a href="/glossary/tripod/">tripod</a> can sometimes create an overhead shot when it has a reversible or horizontal center column. A <a href="/glossary/light-stand/">light stand</a> may be part of a lightweight rig, but it should not be treated as a camera support unless the complete system is designed and secured for that load and angle.</p>

<h3>Why Reach Changes Stability</h3>
<p>Payload ratings are only part of the safety calculation. Extending a camera farther from the support point increases leverage on the arm, joints, clamp, desk, and base. A rig that feels secure with the camera close to the vertical column may flex, droop, or tip when the same camera is moved toward the center of a wide table.</p>
<p>The full load includes the camera body, lens, phone holder, cage, monitor, microphone, battery, cable strain, ball head, quick-release hardware, and any light attached to the same arm.</p>

<h3>What Should You Look for in an Overhead Mount?</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Complete working payload:</strong> Count every mounted component, not only the camera body.</li>
  <li><strong>Required reach:</strong> Measure from the support point to the center of the intended frame.</li>
  <li><strong>Desk compatibility:</strong> For clamps, measure edge thickness, shape, underside clearance, and desktop flex.</li>
  <li><strong>Vertical height:</strong> More height widens the frame but can increase movement and require a wider lens.</li>
  <li><strong>Joint locking:</strong> Check whether pivots, ball heads, and extension sections lock without creeping.</li>
  <li><strong>Mounting interface:</strong> Verify the camera screw, phone holder, quick-release system, and any adapters.</li>
  <li><strong>Counterbalance:</strong> Horizontal arms may require weights or a wider base to oppose the camera load.</li>
  <li><strong>Cable management:</strong> Cables need enough slack for adjustment without pulling the camera.</li>
  <li><strong>Safety tether:</strong> A secondary restraint is valuable when equipment hangs over hands, products, food, or people.</li>
</ul>

<h3>How to Set Up an Overhead Camera Mount</h3>
<ol>
  <li>Define the required frame and measure the subject area before choosing the support position.</li>
  <li>Inspect the desk, floor, wall, or ceiling mounting point for strength and fit.</li>
  <li>Install the support with the arm kept short and the camera position low.</li>
  <li>Add ballast or counterweight according to the support's instructions.</li>
  <li>Attach the camera while supporting it by hand, then engage every safety lock.</li>
  <li>Add a correctly rated safety tether to an independent point when practical.</li>
  <li>Extend the arm and raise the camera gradually, checking flex after each adjustment.</li>
  <li>Route cables along the support with a strain-relief loop near the camera.</li>
  <li>Record a test while using the table normally to identify shake, shadows, glare, and autofocus problems.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Lighting an Overhead Shot</h3>
<p>A camera directly above the work area can cast shadows if a single light sits close to the lens axis. Two diffused lights placed on opposite sides of the work surface can create more even coverage. For reflective products, move the lights and diffusion rather than relying only on exposure changes.</p>
<p>Keep fixtures, stands, and cables outside the camera frame while leaving enough room for hands and tools. Check the monitor for reflections from the camera, arm, ceiling, and operator.</p>

<h3>Common Overhead-Mount Mistakes</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Comparing only payload numbers without considering reach and leverage.</li>
  <li>Clamping to a rounded, hollow, beveled, thin, or obstructed desk edge.</li>
  <li>Attaching the camera and light to the same flexible arm without recalculating the load.</li>
  <li>Using the desk during recording without testing how much it transmits vibration.</li>
  <li>Leaving the camera overhead without a safety tether or regular lock checks.</li>
  <li>Running a tight cable from the camera to a computer or power source.</li>
  <li>Adjusting a loaded boom or arm without supporting the camera.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently Asked Questions</h3>
<h4>Can a regular tripod film straight down?</h4>
<p>Some can. A tripod may need a horizontal, reversible, or lateral center column, or an appropriate boom accessory. The resulting setup must still remain balanced and within the support's limits.</p>

<h4>Are desk-mounted overhead arms stable?</h4>
<p>They can be stable when the desk, clamp, arm length, joints, and load are well matched. Because the mount shares the desk, bumps, typing, cutting, and drawing can still reach the camera.</p>

<h4>How high should an overhead camera be?</h4>
<p>High enough to frame the desired area with the selected lens while preserving useful detail. There is no universal height; sensor size, focal length, table size, and required resolution all affect the answer.</p>

<h4>Do I need a safety tether?</h4>
<p>A secondary restraint is strongly advisable when a camera hangs above people, hands, valuable products, food, or fragile work. It should attach to a reliable point and be rated for the equipment it is protecting.</p>
