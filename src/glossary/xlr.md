<h2>XLR</h2>
<p><strong>XLR</strong> is a family of locking electrical connectors used in professional audio, video production, live events, lighting, intercom, and other equipment. For content creators, "XLR" usually means the familiar <strong>three-pin connector</strong> used to connect a microphone to an <a href="/glossary/audio-interface/">audio interface</a>, mixer, recorder, camera, or preamp.</p>
<p>XLR describes the physical connector and its contact arrangement. It does not by itself describe the microphone type, audio quality, signal level, or whether <a href="/glossary/phantom-power/">phantom power</a> is present.</p>
<p>The connector is popular because it is durable, locks in place, supports common balanced-audio wiring, and has remained compatible across generations of professional equipment.</p>
<h3>What an XLR connector looks like</h3>
<p>The common audio version is round with three contacts and a latch.</p>
<ul>
<li>A <strong>male XLR</strong> connector has exposed pins.</li>
<li>A <strong>female XLR</strong> connector has matching sockets.</li>
<li>A latch or locking tab prevents accidental disconnection.</li>
<li>The shell helps protect the contacts and provides strain relief when properly assembled.</li>
</ul>
<p>Microphones commonly have a male three-pin XLR output, while microphone inputs on mixers and interfaces are commonly female. A standard microphone cable therefore normally has a female connector at the microphone end and a male connector at the receiving-device end.</p>
<p>This convention is common, but creators should still follow equipment labels rather than assuming every XLR-shaped port carries the expected signal.</p>
<h3>What does XLR stand for?</h3>
<p>The name is commonly traced to Cannon Electric's connector development: the X-series design, a later latch version, and a version incorporating a resilient insulating material. The abbreviation is now used generically for the connector family.</p>
<p>Knowing the history is less important than recognizing that XLR is not an audio format or a microphone technology. It is a connector system that can be wired for several purposes.</p>
<h3>Three-pin XLR wiring for balanced audio</h3>
<p>A conventional balanced analog-audio connection uses:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
  <th>Pin</th>
  <th>Typical function</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
  <td>Pin 1</td>
  <td>Shield or ground</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Pin 2</td>
  <td>Positive, hot, or non-inverted signal</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Pin 3</td>
  <td>Negative, cold, or inverted signal</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>The source sends the audio signal in opposite polarity on pins 2 and 3. Interference picked up along the cable tends to affect both conductors similarly. A balanced receiving input compares the two lines, restores the wanted signal, and rejects much of the noise common to both.</p>
<p>This is called <strong>common-mode rejection</strong>. It is one reason balanced XLR connections are useful for long microphone runs, electrically noisy environments, studios, stages, and event production.</p>
<p>The cable and both connected devices must support the intended balanced wiring for the benefit to apply. Simply adding an XLR connector does not convert every signal into balanced audio.</p>
<h3>XLR is a connector, not a guarantee</h3>
<p>An XLR connection does not automatically guarantee:</p>
<ul>
<li>Better sound quality.</li>
<li>A balanced signal.</li>
<li>Microphone-level audio.</li>
<li>+48V phantom power.</li>
<li>A particular impedance.</li>
<li>Analog rather than digital audio.</li>
<li>Compatibility between two devices.</li>
</ul>
<p>Three-pin XLR is strongly associated with balanced microphones, but XLR connectors can also carry line-level audio, AES3 digital audio, intercom, control signals, or manufacturer-specific wiring. XLR families also exist with more than three contacts.</p>
<p>A creator should confirm the port label, manual, signal type, and pinout before connecting unfamiliar equipment.</p>

<p>An XLR cable is not the same thing as the microphone, and an XLR input is not automatically an audio interface. The full signal chain determines how the sound is amplified, converted, monitored, recorded, or streamed.</p>
<h3>Typical creator signal chain</h3>
<p>A common recording setup is:</p>
<p><strong>XLR microphone → XLR cable → microphone preamp/audio interface → USB or Thunderbolt → computer</strong></p>
<p>The components perform different jobs:</p>
<ol>
<li>The microphone converts acoustic sound into a small electrical signal.</li>
<li>The XLR cable carries that signal.</li>
<li>The microphone preamp adds gain.</li>
<li>The interface converts analog audio into digital data.</li>
<li>Recording or streaming software captures the digital input.</li>
</ol>
<p>A mixer, portable recorder, camera, or dedicated XLR-to-USB interface can replace or combine some of these stages.</p>

<p>XLR is not automatically better than <a href="/glossary/usb-microphone/">USB microphone</a> technology. A strong USB microphone can outperform a poor XLR microphone and interface combination. XLR's main advantage is a modular, standardized signal chain with flexible routing and equipment choices.</p>
<h3>XLR vs. TRS</h3>
<p>Both XLR and three-conductor TRS connectors can carry balanced mono audio.</p>

<p>A 1/4-inch TRS connector can also carry stereo unbalanced audio, insert signals, or other wiring. As with XLR, the connector shape alone does not identify the exact signal.</p>
<h3>XLR and phantom power</h3>
<p>Many audio interfaces and mixers can send DC power through a balanced XLR microphone cable to operate compatible <a href="/glossary/condenser-microphones/">condenser microphones</a> and active circuitry. The common studio value is +48V.</p>
<p>Important distinctions:</p>
<ul>
<li>XLR does not mean phantom power is active.</li>
<li>An XLR input may have no phantom-power capability.</li>
<li>Phantom power may be switched globally, by channel group, or per input.</li>
<li><a href="/glossary/dynamic-microphones/">Dynamic microphones</a> usually do not need phantom power.</li>
<li>Some active dynamic, inline preamp, and condenser designs do require it.</li>
<li>Unbalanced, damaged, incorrectly wired, or sensitive vintage equipment requires caution.</li>
</ul>
<p>Creators should check the microphone and receiving-device manuals instead of applying phantom power based only on the connector.</p>
<h3>Microphone level, line level, and gain</h3>
<p>A typical microphone produces a low-level signal that requires a microphone preamp. An XLR input labeled <strong>Mic</strong> is designed to provide that gain.</p>
<p>An XLR connection can also carry a stronger <strong>line-level</strong> signal from a mixer, wireless receiver, preamp, or other device. Sending line level into a microphone input with excessive gain can cause clipping. Sending mic level into a line input can produce an extremely quiet recording.</p>
<p>Combo inputs may switch behavior depending on whether the creator inserts XLR or 1/4-inch TRS. The labels and manual determine the intended source.</p>
<h3>Can an XLR-to-USB cable connect a microphone directly?</h3>
<p>A conventional analog XLR microphone cannot become a USB device through a purely passive cable. The setup needs:</p>
<ul>
<li>Microphone preamplification.</li>
<li>Analog-to-digital conversion.</li>
<li>USB communication.</li>
<li>Phantom power when required.</li>
</ul>
<p>Some products look like cables but contain active electronics in the USB end. Their gain, noise, latency, sample-rate support, monitoring, and phantom-power capabilities vary.</p>
<p>A proper audio interface or active XLR-to-USB interface is normally the reliable choice for recording and streaming.</p>
<h3>Choosing an XLR cable</h3>
<p>For ordinary creator setups, look for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Three-pin male-to-female microphone cable construction.</li>
<li>Balanced two-conductor cable with shield.</li>
<li>Reliable connectors and strain relief.</li>
<li>Appropriate length without excessive unused cable.</li>
<li>Flexible jacket for desk or boom-arm routing.</li>
<li>Clear labeling when several cables are used.</li>
</ul>
<p>Expensive cable materials do not automatically transform the microphone's sound. Mechanical reliability, shielding, connector quality, and correct wiring usually matter more than extravagant marketing claims.</p>
<h3>Troubleshooting an XLR connection</h3>
<p>If the microphone is silent or noisy, check:</p>
<ol>
<li>The microphone is connected to a microphone input rather than an output.</li>
<li>The correct input is selected in the software.</li>
<li>Gain is raised to an appropriate level.</li>
<li>Phantom power is enabled only when the connected device requires it.</li>
<li>The cable is fully latched at both ends.</li>
<li>The interface or mixer is powered and recognized.</li>
<li>Monitoring is routed correctly.</li>
<li>The cable works with another known-good microphone.</li>
<li>The microphone works with another known-good input.</li>
<li>No pad, mute, gate, or channel switch is blocking the signal.</li>
</ol>
<p>A loud hum can indicate cable damage, grounding issues, unbalanced adaptation, or interference. Intermittent audio often points to a damaged connector, strain relief, solder joint, or cable conductor.</p>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Is XLR better than USB?</h4>
<p>Not automatically. XLR offers modularity, balanced cable runs, professional routing, and easier multi-microphone expansion. USB offers a simpler all-in-one connection. Sound quality depends on the complete system and recording environment.</p>
<h4>Does every XLR microphone need phantom power?</h4>
<p>No. Many condenser microphones need phantom power, while most passive dynamic microphones do not. Some active dynamic and specialty designs have different requirements.</p>
<h4>Can XLR carry stereo audio?</h4>
<p>A single conventional three-pin XLR connection normally carries one balanced mono channel. Stereo commonly uses two XLR connections, although specialized wiring and higher-pin-count connectors exist.</p>
<h4>Are all three-pin XLR ports compatible?</h4>
<p>No. Three-pin XLR is commonly used for balanced audio, but the same connector shape can carry other signals or nonstandard wiring. Check the equipment documentation.</p>
<h4>Why do XLR cables lock?</h4>
<p>The latch helps prevent accidental disconnection during recording, live performance, movement, or cable strain.</p>
