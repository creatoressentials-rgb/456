<h2>Overhead Shot</h2>
<p>An <strong>overhead shot</strong> is filmed with the camera positioned directly or nearly directly above the subject and pointed downward. It creates a top-down view that clearly shows the arrangement, movement, or relationship of objects across a surface.</p>
<p>Overhead shots are common in cooking, crafts, drawing, repairs, unboxings, tabletop games, product layouts, fitness instruction, and stylized storytelling.</p>

<h3>Overhead Shot vs. High-Angle and Aerial Shots</h3>
<table><thead><tr><th>Shot</th><th>Camera Position</th><th>Typical Use</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>High-angle shot</td><td>Above the subject and looking downward at any meaningful angle</td><td>Shows layout, scale, or a downward perspective</td></tr>
<tr><td>Overhead shot</td><td>Directly or nearly directly above, often near 90 degrees</td><td>Maps a surface, action, or arrangement</td></tr>
<tr><td>Bird's-eye view</td><td>Often used as another name for overhead, though usage varies</td><td>Top-down visual perspective</td></tr>
<tr><td>Aerial shot</td><td>Captured from a substantially elevated platform, aircraft, or drone</td><td>Large locations, geography, and scale</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>An overhead shot is a specific form of high-angle view. It is not automatically aerial, and it does not require a drone.</p>

<h3>Why Use an Overhead Shot?</h3>
<ul>
<li>Show every item on a table without one object blocking another.</li>
<li>Make hand movements easy to follow.</li>
<li>Reveal patterns, symmetry, and spatial relationships.</li>
<li>Keep a product demonstration visually organized.</li>
<li>Show steps in a recipe, craft, repair, or assembly process.</li>
<li>Create a graphic flat-lay composition.</li>
<li>Show movement across a floor, court, or game board.</li>
</ul>

<h3>How to Mount an Overhead Camera</h3>
<p>Common supports include an <a href="/glossary/overhead-camera-mount/">overhead camera mount</a>, boom arm, C-stand, wall or ceiling mount, horizontal tripod arm, or a securely rigged production support.</p>
<ul>
<li>Confirm that the support's payload covers the camera, lens, phone holder, cables, and accessories.</li>
<li>Account for leverage when the horizontal arm is extended.</li>
<li>Use counterweights, sandbags, safety cables, and secondary retention where appropriate.</li>
<li>Keep the support outside the working area when possible.</li>
<li>Route power and data cables so they cannot pull the camera down.</li>
<li>Never place an inadequately secured camera over a person, food, fragile product, or client.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Overhead Shot With a Phone</h3>
<p>A phone can produce an effective top-down view using a compatible <a href="/glossary/phone-mount/">phone mount</a> and a stable overhead support. Confirm that the clamp cannot press buttons, release unexpectedly, or rotate under the phone's weight.</p>
<p>Lock orientation, focus, exposure, and white balance when automatic changes would be distracting.</p>

<h3>How to Compose an Overhead Shot</h3>
<ol>
<li>Mark the visible edges of the frame on the table or work surface.</li>
<li>Place the most important action where hands and tools will not hide it.</li>
<li>Keep frequently used objects within easy reach but outside the frame until needed.</li>
<li>Align the camera sensor with the work surface to reduce unintended keystone distortion.</li>
<li>Use deliberate symmetry, diagonal movement, or negative space.</li>
<li>Leave crop-safe room when horizontal and vertical versions are planned.</li>
<li>Record a realistic rehearsal and check where arms enter the frame.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Lighting an Overhead Shot</h3>
<p>A light mounted near the camera can create flat illumination, while one light from the side can cast distracting hand shadows. A balanced setup often uses two soft lights from opposite sides at angles that keep the camera and operator shadows out of the scene.</p>
<ul>
<li>Use large diffusion to soften reflections and hand shadows.</li>
<li>Angle reflective products so lights and the camera do not appear in them.</li>
<li>Match the color temperature of all active light sources.</li>
<li>Turn off mixed household lighting when it creates color contamination.</li>
<li>Check that the camera or support does not block the light.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Focus, Exposure, and Distortion</h3>
<p>The work surface should remain within the lens's usable focus range. If hands rise substantially above the table, use enough depth of field to keep both the surface and action acceptably sharp.</p>
<p>A wide lens can cover a large table from a short height but may stretch objects near the edges. Raising the camera and using a less extreme focal length can create more even-looking proportions when the room allows it.</p>

<h3>Overhead Shot vs. Point-of-View Shot</h3>
<p>An overhead shot describes the camera's physical angle. A point-of-view shot represents what a person or participant sees. A top-down shot can be a POV when it convincingly represents someone looking down, but most tabletop overhead shots are objective instructional views.</p>

<h3>Common Overhead-Shot Mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Calling every high-angle image an overhead shot.</li>
<li>Mounting a camera above people without proper safety measures.</li>
<li>Ignoring the increased leverage of a long horizontal arm.</li>
<li>Using one hard side light that creates severe hand shadows.</li>
<li>Letting hands, sleeves, or hair block the demonstrated action.</li>
<li>Using a lens so wide that objects distort badly near the edges.</li>
<li>Failing to mark the visible work area before filming.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently Asked Questions</h3>
<h4>Is an overhead shot always exactly 90 degrees?</h4>
<p>It is commonly direct or nearly direct top-down. A visibly shallower view may be better described as a high-angle shot.</p>
<h4>Do you need a special overhead camera mount?</h4>
<p>No single support is required, but the system must hold the complete rig safely and resist rotation, vibration, cable pull, and tipping.</p>
<h4>Can an overhead shot show a person's face?</h4>
<p>It can, but the angle is usually better for layout, body position, or action than for natural facial communication.</p>
<h4>Why is my overhead footage crooked?</h4>
<p>The camera may not be parallel to the work surface, the support may be rotating, or the lens may introduce distortion. Use grid lines and align the camera before recording.</p>
