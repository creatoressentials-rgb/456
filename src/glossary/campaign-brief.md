<h2>Campaign Brief</h2>
<p>A <strong>campaign brief</strong> is a document that explains the plan and expectations for a marketing campaign. In a creator partnership, it aligns the brand, agency, and creator on the campaign’s objective, audience, message, <a href="/glossary/deliverables/">deliverables</a>, schedule, requirements, and measures of success.</p>
<p>It answers the high-level questions: <strong>Why is the campaign happening? Who should it reach? What needs to be delivered? When will it run? What outcome matters?</strong></p>
<p>Brands may call the same or a similar document an <strong>influencer brief</strong>, <strong>creator brief</strong>, <strong>partnership brief</strong>, or <strong>campaign overview</strong>. The terminology is not standardized. The substance of the document matters more than its label.</p>
<h3>What a creator campaign brief should include</h3>
<h4>Campaign background</h4>
<p>Provide enough context for the creator to understand the company, product, launch, problem, or opportunity. Avoid turning the brief into a full company history. The creator needs the information that affects the content.</p>
<p>Useful background can include:</p>
<ul>
<li>What the brand offers</li>
<li>Why the campaign is happening now</li>
<li>The product or service being promoted</li>
<li>The market problem or customer need</li>
<li>Previous campaign lessons</li>
<li>Important competitive or category context</li>
</ul>
<h4>Campaign objective</h4>
<p>State the primary business or communication goal. Examples include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Build awareness for a product launch</li>
<li>Generate qualified traffic</li>
<li>Drive tracked purchases or sign-ups</li>
<li>Explain a difficult product feature</li>
<li>Reach a new audience segment</li>
<li>Collect reusable creator assets</li>
<li>Improve trust through demonstrations or testimonials</li>
</ul>
<p>A brief with five competing primary goals usually produces unfocused content. Identify the most important outcome and any secondary goals.</p>
<h4>Target audience</h4>
<p>Describe the people the content should reach in practical terms. Demographics may help, but motivations, problems, objections, and viewing behavior are often more actionable.</p>
<p>For example, “U.S. adults ages 25–44” is less useful than “new freelance designers who need simple invoicing but are intimidated by accounting software.”</p>
<h4>Campaign message and offer</h4>
<p>Clarify the main message viewers should understand and the action they should take. Include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Core value proposition</li>
<li>Required product facts</li>
<li>Current price or promotion</li>
<li>Discount code or <a href="/glossary/tracking-link/">tracking link</a></li>
<li>Call to action</li>
<li>Approved proof points</li>
<li>Important limitations or eligibility rules</li>
<li>Prohibited or unsupported claims</li>
</ul>
<p>The creator should be allowed to describe genuine experience honestly. A brief should never require a creator to claim results or opinions they did not have.</p>
<h4>Deliverables and channels</h4>
<p>List each required asset precisely:</p>
<ul>
<li>Platform and account</li>
<li>Video, Short, post, livestream, image, or story format</li>
<li>Quantity</li>
<li>Approximate duration</li>
<li><a href="/glossary/sponsorship/">Sponsorship</a> placement</li>
<li>Caption, link, tag, and hashtag requirements</li>
<li>Thumbnail or cover requirements</li>
<li>Raw-footage or alternate-version requests</li>
<li>Accessibility requirements</li>
<li>Publication and availability period</li>
</ul>
<p>“Make three social posts” is vague. “One 45–60 second vertical video for <a href="/glossary/youtube-shorts/">YouTube Shorts</a> and two cutdowns with different opening hooks” gives the creator something operational.</p>
<h4><a href="/glossary/timeline/">Timeline</a> and workflow</h4>
<p>Include dates for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Product delivery or account access</li>
<li>Concept submission</li>
<li>Draft submission</li>
<li>Brand feedback</li>
<li>Revision delivery</li>
<li>Final approval</li>
<li>Publication window</li>
<li>Reporting or invoice submission</li>
</ul>
<p>The schedule should include realistic review time. If brand feedback arrives late, the publication date may need to move.</p>
<h4>Creative guardrails</h4>
<p>A campaign brief can state the broad content approach, while a <a href="/glossary/creative-brief/">creative brief</a> supplies more detailed execution guidance. Campaign-level guardrails may include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Desired tone</li>
<li>Topics to emphasize or avoid</li>
<li>Visual identity requirements</li>
<li>Mandatory wording</li>
<li>Safety or regulatory requirements</li>
<li>Competitors that cannot appear</li>
<li>Music, location, wardrobe, or product-display restrictions</li>
<li>Examples of on-brand and off-brand content</li>
</ul>
<p>Guardrails should protect the campaign without erasing the creator’s voice. Content that sounds unlike the creator can weaken viewer trust and performance.</p>
<h4>Measurement and reporting</h4>
<p>Define which metrics matter and when they will be evaluated. Depending on the goal, these may include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Views or reach</li>
<li>Watch time and <a href="/glossary/retention/">retention</a></li>
<li><a href="/glossary/click-through-rate/">Click-through rate</a></li>
<li>Engagement</li>
<li>Code uses or affiliate sales</li>
<li>Leads, installs, or registrations</li>
<li>Cost per result</li>
<li>Brand-lift research</li>
<li>Quantity and usability of delivered assets</li>
</ul>
<p>State the <a href="/glossary/attribution/">attribution</a> window and data source when performance payment depends on results. A creator’s live dashboard, the brand’s analytics, and an affiliate platform may report different totals.</p>
<h4>Compliance and disclosure</h4>
<p>The brief should explain the required platform declaration and viewer-facing disclosure. In the United States, the FTC says disclosures should be hard to miss, understandable, and placed with the endorsement itself. A vague instruction such as “follow all laws” is not a useful operating guide.</p>
<p>The brief should also identify who approved product claims and where the supporting evidence is stored. Health, finance, environmental, earnings, and comparative claims may require additional review.</p>

<p>The documents can refer to one another. For example, a contract may state that the attached brief defines the approved deliverables. Creators should not assume the brief alone resolves payment, ownership, licensing, cancellation, or exclusivity.</p>
<h3>Campaign brief vs. creative brief</h3>
<p>In practice, the two terms often overlap. A useful distinction is:</p>
<ul>
<li>The <strong>campaign brief</strong> explains the overall marketing plan and desired result.</li>
<li>The <strong>creative brief</strong> translates that plan into direction for a specific piece or group of content.</li>
</ul>
<p>A small <a href="/glossary/brand-deal/">brand deal</a> may use one combined document. A larger campaign involving many creators, paid media, landing pages, and multiple formats may have a master campaign brief plus separate creative briefs.</p>
<h3>What creators should clarify before production</h3>
<p>A creator should resolve any material ambiguity before filming or editing:</p>
<ol>
<li>What is the primary objective?</li>
<li>Which deliverables are guaranteed?</li>
<li>Which statements are mandatory, and which are examples?</li>
<li>Can the creator give honest negative or mixed feedback?</li>
<li>Who approves the concept and final content?</li>
<li>How many revision rounds are included?</li>
<li>What happens if the product arrives late or does not work?</li>
<li>Where will the brand use the content?</li>
<li>Will the content become a paid advertisement?</li>
<li>Is there <a href="/glossary/category-exclusivity/">category exclusivity</a>?</li>
<li>What disclosure wording is required?</li>
<li>Which metrics affect payment?</li>
</ol>
<p>The answers that affect money, rights, or risk belong in the agreement—not only in an email thread.</p>
<h3>How brands can write a better creator brief</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Lead with the objective:</strong> Give the creator a clear problem to solve.</li>
<li><strong>Separate must-haves from suggestions:</strong> Creators need to know what is negotiable.</li>
<li><strong>Use accurate product facts:</strong> Provide evidence and current terms.</li>
<li><strong>Show examples without demanding copies:</strong> References should communicate direction, not encourage imitation.</li>
<li><strong>Limit mandatory talking points:</strong> A long checklist often produces unnatural content.</li>
<li><strong>Name one decision-maker:</strong> Conflicting feedback creates delays.</li>
<li><strong>Define revision limits:</strong> Feedback should correct agreed requirements rather than redesign the entire concept.</li>
<li><strong>Allow disclosure space:</strong> Do not ask the creator to hide the commercial relationship.</li>
<li><strong>Respect the creator’s audience knowledge:</strong> The creator understands how viewers normally respond to the channel.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Common campaign-brief mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>No measurable objective:</strong> The team cannot evaluate success consistently.</li>
<li><strong>A vague audience:</strong> The content speaks to everyone and persuades no one.</li>
<li><strong>Contradictory instructions:</strong> “Be completely authentic” conflicts with a word-for-word script.</li>
<li><strong>Hidden deliverables:</strong> Raw footage, cutdowns, and paid-ad rights appear late in the process.</li>
<li><strong>Unrealistic deadlines:</strong> The schedule ignores shipping, review, filming, or revisions.</li>
<li><strong>Unsupported claims:</strong> The creator is asked to repeat claims without evidence.</li>
<li><strong>No approval owner:</strong> Several stakeholders provide incompatible feedback.</li>
<li><strong>Treating the brief as the contract:</strong> Payment and licensing remain unresolved.</li>
<li><strong>Using stale information:</strong> Old prices, links, product names, and offers create publication errors.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Is a campaign brief legally binding?</h4>
<p>Not automatically. It is primarily a planning and alignment document. It may become part of a binding agreement if the contract incorporates it, but creators should confirm the actual contract terms.</p>
<h4>Who writes a campaign brief?</h4>
<p>The brand, agency, campaign manager, or creator-partnerships team usually prepares it. Strong briefs are refined with input from creative, legal, product, measurement, and the creator when appropriate.</p>
<h4>How long should a campaign brief be?</h4>
<p>There is no required length. It should be concise enough to use while containing every material instruction. A one-page brief may work for a simple integration; a multi-market launch may need more detail and attachments.</p>
<h4>Is a campaign brief the same as a creator media kit?</h4>
<p>No. The brand sends a campaign brief to explain the opportunity. The creator sends a <a href="/glossary/creator-media-kit/">creator media kit</a> to demonstrate <a href="/glossary/audience-fit/">audience fit</a>, performance, services, and experience.</p>
<h4>Can a campaign brief change after signing?</h4>
<p>It can, but changes to deliverables, timing, rights, or workload may require a written contract amendment and additional compensation.</p>
