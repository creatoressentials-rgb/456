<h2>Medium Shot</h2>
<p>A <strong>medium shot</strong> is a camera shot that shows a person at an intermediate scale—commonly from around the waist upward—while preserving enough background to provide context. It balances facial expression with gestures and body language.</p>
<p>The exact crop varies by production and subject. Medium shots are also called mid-shots, and the label may be used more broadly for framing that sits between a wide shot and a close-up.</p>

<h3>What Does a Medium Shot Show?</h3>
<ul>
  <li>The subject's face and upper-body expression</li>
  <li>Hand gestures and interaction with nearby objects</li>
  <li>Some clothing, posture, and body language</li>
  <li>Enough environment to understand the location</li>
  <li>A comfortable conversational distance for viewers</li>
</ul>
<p>Because it preserves expression and context, the medium shot is common in interviews, tutorials, dialogue, presentations, product demonstrations, and <a href="/glossary/talking-head-video/">talking-head videos</a>.</p>

<p>These boundaries are conventions, not fixed measurements. Different productions and teaching sources may place the lower crop slightly differently.</p>

<h3>Is a Medium Shot Always Waist-Up?</h3>
<p>No. Waist-up is the most common shorthand, but some sources use medium shot more loosely for a tighter scene view or a person cropped between full length and close-up.</p>
<p>Rather than relying only on the term, a <a href="/glossary/shot-list/">shot list</a> can specify "waist-up medium," "seated medium," or another practical description.</p>

<h3>Why Is the Medium Shot So Common?</h3>
<p>The medium shot resembles a comfortable conversational distance. It is close enough for viewers to read the face but wide enough to show hands, posture, nearby objects, and part of the setting.</p>
<p>It is also flexible in editing. A high-resolution source may permit a modest crop to a medium close-up, while the original frame still provides wider context.</p>

<h3>How YouTube Creators Use Medium Shots</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Educational videos:</strong> Show the presenter and natural hand gestures.</li>
  <li><strong>Reviews:</strong> Let the host hold and discuss a product.</li>
  <li><strong>Interviews:</strong> Preserve facial expression and body language.</li>
  <li><strong>Cooking and crafts:</strong> Show the presenter with the nearby work area.</li>
  <li><strong>Comedy and sketches:</strong> Capture reactions and physical performance together.</li>
  <li><strong>Video podcasts:</strong> Frame seated participants without becoming excessively wide.</li>
  <li><strong>Livestreams:</strong> Provide a stable view that tolerates normal posture changes.</li>
</ul>

<h3>How to Frame a Medium Shot</h3>
<ol>
  <li>Place the camera at an intentional height, often near eye level for a neutral presentation.</li>
  <li>Choose a lower crop that avoids cutting awkwardly through hands, wrists, elbows, or other major joints.</li>
  <li>Leave enough room for the gestures the subject will actually make.</li>
  <li>Set appropriate headroom and eye placement.</li>
  <li>Check that background objects do not intersect the subject's head or shoulders.</li>
  <li>Use camera distance and focal length that create natural-looking perspective.</li>
  <li>Ask the presenter to perform a realistic movement test before recording.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Medium Shots and Hand Gestures</h3>
<p>A common mistake is framing a presenter at the waist while their hands repeatedly leave the image. Watch the natural gesture range during rehearsal.</p>
<p>If the subject uses broad gestures or demonstrates an object, widen the frame or provide a second angle. If gestures are minimal, a tighter medium shot may feel more focused.</p>

<h3>Medium Shots for Seated Subjects</h3>
<p>Waist-up language becomes less useful when a person is seated behind a desk or table. Frame based on visible posture, hands, furniture, and the action rather than trying to preserve an anatomical crop that the set already hides.</p>
<p>Check whether the table edge cuts through the subject awkwardly and whether important products remain visible.</p>

<h3>Lens and Perspective</h3>
<p>A medium shot can be created with many focal lengths. The important distinction is that camera distance changes perspective.</p>
<p>A wide lens placed very close may enlarge the face, hands, or objects nearest the camera. Moving back and using a longer focal length often creates a more natural portrait perspective, although the best choice depends on room size and visual style.</p>

<h3>Medium Shots in Editing</h3>
<p>Medium shots often connect wide shots and close-ups within a sequence. They provide enough context to orient the viewer while preserving more emotional detail than a distant frame.</p>
<p>For interviews and demonstrations, combine medium coverage with close-ups, inserts, screen recordings, or B-roll when viewers need to see fine detail.</p>

<h3>Common Medium-Shot Mistakes</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Treating waist-up as an exact universal boundary.</li>
  <li>Cropping through hands, wrists, or elbows during natural gestures.</li>
  <li>Placing a wide-angle phone too close to the face.</li>
  <li>Leaving so much background that the result functions more like a wide shot.</li>
  <li>Framing so tightly that the subject cannot move naturally.</li>
  <li>Assuming the medium shot alone provides enough detail for every demonstration.</li>
  <li>Ignoring how a desk, chair, or product changes the practical crop.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently Asked Questions</h3>
<h4>Where does a medium shot crop a person?</h4>
<p>Commonly around the waist, but exact practice varies. The crop should preserve the gestures and body language required by the scene.</p>

<h4>Is a medium shot the same as a medium close-up?</h4>
<p>No. A medium close-up is tighter, commonly framing the person from around the chest or shoulders upward.</p>

<h4>What lens should be used for a medium shot?</h4>
<p>There is no required focal length. Choose the lens and camera distance that provide the desired perspective and fit the available space.</p>

<h4>Why use a medium shot instead of a close-up?</h4>
<p>A medium shot shows more gestures, posture, props, and environment while still keeping the face readable.</p>
