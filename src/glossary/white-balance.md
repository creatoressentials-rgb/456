<h2>White Balance</h2>
<p><strong>White balance</strong> is a camera or editing adjustment that controls how colors are reproduced under different light sources. Its basic goal is to make neutral objects—such as white paper or a gray card—appear neutral rather than blue, orange, green, or magenta.</p>
<p>Human vision adapts quickly to changing light. A white wall can still seem white under daylight, a household lamp, or shade. A camera records the color of those sources more literally and needs a reference for what should count as neutral.</p>
<p>White balance is closely related to <a href="/glossary/color-temperature/">color temperature</a>, but they are not the same concept. Color temperature describes the light source. White balance is the correction the camera or software applies in response.</p>
<h3>Why lighting changes recorded color</h3>
<p>Different light sources distribute visible wavelengths differently.</p>
<ul>
<li>Candlelight and many tungsten bulbs appear warm or amber.</li>
<li>Midday daylight is comparatively neutral.</li>
<li>Open shade and overcast light can appear cooler or bluer.</li>
<li>Fluorescent and some LED fixtures can add green or magenta casts that a simple Kelvin setting does not fully correct.</li>
</ul>
<p>Without suitable white balance, skin tones, products, walls, and brand colors may look inconsistent. This is especially noticeable when a video cuts between cameras or between shots recorded at different times.</p>
<h3>How white balance works</h3>
<p>A camera balances color by changing the relative response of its red, green, and blue channels. If the light source is very warm, the camera adds a cooler correction. If the source is cool, it adds a warmer correction.</p>
<p>This leads to a common point of confusion:</p>
<ul>
<li>A <strong>higher color-temperature light source</strong> is described as cooler or bluer.</li>
<li>A <strong>higher Kelvin white-balance setting on the camera</strong>, under unchanged light, usually makes the recorded image warmer because the camera assumes it must compensate for a cooler source.</li>
</ul>
<p>The setting tells the camera what kind of light it should neutralize. It is not a direct "make the image this color" control.</p>

<p>For a fixed studio or talking-head setup, a locked preset, Kelvin value, or custom measurement usually provides more consistent video than auto white balance.</p>
<h3>Auto white balance in video</h3>
<p>Auto white balance can work well for casual recording, mobile video, or a creator moving through rapidly changing environments. The risk is that the camera may recalculate its decision while the shot is still recording.</p>
<p>A bright colored object entering the frame, a monitor changing content, or the creator moving closer to the camera can cause the image to drift warmer, cooler, greener, or more magenta. These shifts are difficult to hide in a continuous take.</p>
<p>A practical approach is:</p>
<ul>
<li>Use auto white balance when lighting genuinely changes and speed matters more than perfect consistency.</li>
<li>Lock white balance when the light is controlled or a continuous shot must match from beginning to end.</li>
<li>Rebalance intentionally when moving to a new room, time of day, or lighting setup.</li>
</ul>

<p>Many cameras allow direct Kelvin entry, often within a range such as approximately 2500K to 10000K. A creator can match the camera setting to the dominant light source and then adjust for the preferred look.</p>

<p>To understand the direction of adjustment under unchanged light:</p>
<ul>
<li>If the image looks too blue, raise the camera's Kelvin setting to warm it.</li>
<li>If the image looks too orange, lower the setting to cool it.</li>
</ul>
<h3>Custom white balance and gray cards</h3>
<p>A custom white balance uses a neutral target photographed or measured under the same light that falls on the subject. Many cameras can store that measurement as a custom setting.</p>
<p>A neutral gray card is often preferable to an ordinary sheet of paper because paper may contain optical brighteners or a subtle color cast. The reference should:</p>
<ol>
<li>Be placed where the subject will be.</li>
<li>Receive the same light as the subject.</li>
<li>Fill the required portion of the frame according to the camera's instructions.</li>
<li>Avoid clipped highlights or deep underexposure.</li>
<li>Be remeasured if the light changes.</li>
</ol>
<p>A color checker provides additional patches for color correction and matching, but it does not replace good exposure or consistent lighting.</p>
<h3>Temperature vs. tint</h3>
<p>White balance has two major correction directions:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Temperature:</strong> Blue to amber.</li>
<li><strong>Tint:</strong> Green to magenta.</li>
</ul>
<p>Kelvin controls primarily describe the blue-amber axis. A green cast from fluorescent lighting or an inexpensive LED may remain even when the Kelvin number appears correct. Cameras and editing applications may provide a separate white-balance shift, tint control, or custom measurement to address it.</p>
<p>Creators matching several cameras should compare both temperature and tint. Two cameras set to 5600K can still look different because of sensor response, picture profiles, lenses, internal processing, and the lights themselves.</p>
<h3>Mixed lighting</h3>
<p>Mixed lighting occurs when the subject is illuminated by sources with different color characteristics, such as daylight from a window and a warm ceiling lamp.</p>
<p>One camera setting cannot perfectly neutralize both sources at once. If the camera is balanced for the window, the lamp-lit areas may remain orange. If it is balanced for the lamp, the window light may appear blue.</p>
<p>Better solutions include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Turning off unnecessary fixtures.</li>
<li>Blocking or controlling window light.</li>
<li>Using matching bulbs or video lights.</li>
<li>Adding color-correction gels to a light.</li>
<li>Positioning the subject so one source becomes dominant.</li>
<li>Accepting a deliberate warm or cool background while keeping skin tones controlled.</li>
</ul>
<p>White balance corrects the camera's response; it does not make mismatched light sources physically identical.</p>
<h3>White balance for YouTube talking-head videos</h3>
<p>A repeatable creator workflow is:</p>
<ol>
<li>Set the <a href="/glossary/three-point-lighting/">three-point lighting</a> or other studio lights to their intended output.</li>
<li>Turn off or control competing room lights and daylight.</li>
<li>Place the creator in the final position.</li>
<li>Set exposure before measuring the reference.</li>
<li>Choose a fixed Kelvin value or create a custom white balance.</li>
<li>Record a short test with skin tones, white, gray, and any important product colors.</li>
<li>Lock the setting for the full take.</li>
<li>Save the camera and light settings for the next session.</li>
</ol>
<p>Consistency matters more than chasing an exact universal number. A slightly warm image that remains stable is often easier to grade than a technically neutral image that changes during every sentence.</p>
<h3>White balance in post-production</h3>
<p>Editors can adjust white balance using temperature, tint, curves, channel controls, or a neutral-color eyedropper. Correction is easiest when the recording retains enough color information.</p>
<p>Greater flexibility generally comes from:</p>
<ul>
<li>RAW capture.</li>
<li>Higher bit depth.</li>
<li>Less aggressive compression.</li>
<li>Proper exposure.</li>
<li>A recorded gray or color reference.</li>
</ul>
<p>Heavily compressed 8-bit video can break apart when pushed far, producing banding, noise, strange skin tones, or clipped channels. Recording a wildly incorrect white balance and planning to "fix it later" is riskier than getting close in camera.</p>
<p>White balance adjustment also cannot fully repair mixed lighting that places different color casts across the same face or product.</p>
<h3>Neutral color vs. creative color</h3>
<p>The technically neutral setting is not always the final creative choice. A creator may preserve warm sunset light, make a nighttime scene cooler, or use contrasting warm and cool sources for depth.</p>
<p>The important distinction is between:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Accidental color cast:</strong> The camera misrepresents the intended scene.</li>
<li><strong>Deliberate color design:</strong> The creator chooses a look consistently.</li>
</ul>
<p>A reliable workflow first establishes a controlled neutral reference, then makes intentional creative changes.</p>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>What white balance should I use for YouTube videos?</h4>
<p>Use a fixed setting that matches the dominant light in the recording space. Daylight-balanced creator lights often start near 5000K–5600K, while tungsten lighting often starts near 3200K. Test the actual setup rather than relying only on the label.</p>
<h4>Is auto white balance bad for video?</h4>
<p>Not always. It is useful when lighting changes quickly, but it can create visible shifts during a continuous shot. Controlled studio videos usually benefit from a locked setting.</p>
<h4>Why does increasing Kelvin make my image warmer?</h4>
<p>The camera assumes the light source is cooler and applies a warmer correction. The Kelvin value describes the source the camera is compensating for, not a direct output color.</p>
<h4>Can white balance be fixed after recording?</h4>
<p>Often, within limits. RAW and high-bit-depth footage offer more flexibility. Strong errors, mixed lighting, clipped channels, and heavily compressed video can be difficult or impossible to correct cleanly.</p>
<h4>Should I use a white card or gray card?</h4>
<p>A calibrated neutral gray card is usually more reliable than ordinary white paper. Follow the camera's instructions, because some custom white-balance systems specify a particular target or exposure method.</p>
