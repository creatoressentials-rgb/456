<h2>Creative Brief</h2>
<p>A <strong>creative brief</strong> is a concise document that translates a project’s strategy into practical direction for the people creating the content. In a creator partnership, it explains what the video or post should communicate, who it should reach, what it should feel like, which elements are required, and what the audience should do next.</p>
<p>The creative brief connects the marketing goal to the actual concept, script, filming, editing, graphics, and call to action. It should provide enough direction to align the brand and creator without turning every line into a rigid script.</p>
<h3>What a creative brief does</h3>
<p>A useful brief helps answer:</p>
<ul>
<li>What is the purpose of this content?</li>
<li>Who is the intended viewer?</li>
<li>What single message should the viewer remember?</li>
<li>What should the viewer feel, understand, or do?</li>
<li>Which facts, visuals, disclosures, and calls to action are mandatory?</li>
<li>What tone and format fit the campaign?</li>
<li>What should the content avoid?</li>
<li>How will the work be reviewed and approved?</li>
</ul>
<p>Adobe’s current creative-brief guidance uses video production as an example, identifying the video’s purpose, audience, message, style, distribution, length, format, and production requirements as relevant inputs.</p>
<h3>What to include in a creator creative brief</h3>
<h4>Creative objective</h4>
<p>Describe the communication problem the asset should solve. A useful objective is specific enough to guide creative decisions.</p>
<p>Weak: “Make an engaging video about our app.”</p>
<p>Stronger: “Show first-time freelancers how the app turns a photographed receipt into an organized expense in under one minute.”</p>
<p>The objective should support the wider <a href="/glossary/campaign-brief/">campaign brief</a> while remaining focused on the individual asset.</p>
<h4>Target audience and viewer insight</h4>
<p>Identify the viewer and the belief, concern, or need the content should address. Useful insights may include:</p>
<ul>
<li>What the viewer already knows</li>
<li>What problem the viewer feels</li>
<li>Why the viewer has not acted</li>
<li>What language the viewer uses</li>
<li>What proof the viewer needs</li>
<li>What normally earns attention on the creator’s channel</li>
</ul>
<p>A demographic list without motivation may not give the creator enough information to build a compelling concept.</p>
<h4>Single-minded message</h4>
<p>Choose the main idea the audience should remember. Supporting points can reinforce it, but they should not compete with it.</p>
<p>The message is not necessarily the exact sentence the creator must speak. It is the meaning the content should communicate.</p>
<h4>Required proof and product facts</h4>
<p>Provide accurate, current information such as:</p>
<ul>
<li>Product name and pronunciation</li>
<li>Demonstrable features</li>
<li>Eligibility and geographic limits</li>
<li>Price, trial, or promotion terms</li>
<li>Approved comparisons</li>
<li>Evidence supporting measurable claims</li>
<li>Required disclaimers</li>
<li>Expiration dates</li>
</ul>
<p>The creator should not be told to claim personal results that did not happen or repeat objective claims the advertiser cannot substantiate.</p>
<h4>Tone and creator voice</h4>
<p>Describe the desired feeling using specific, compatible guidance. “Confident and practical, with light humor” is more useful than a list of ten contradictory adjectives.</p>
<p>The brand can identify the tone while allowing the creator to speak naturally. A word-for-word corporate script may sound unlike the channel and weaken the credibility the brand is paying to access.</p>
<h4>Format and distribution</h4>
<p>Specify the production context:</p>
<ul>
<li>YouTube video, Short, livestream, post, or another format</li>
<li>Landscape, square, or vertical <a href="/glossary/aspect-ratio/">aspect ratio</a></li>
<li>Approximate length</li>
<li><a href="/glossary/sponsorship/">Sponsorship</a> placement</li>
<li>Caption and link requirements</li>
<li>Safe areas for a <a href="/glossary/lower-third/">lower third</a>, subtitles, and interface elements</li>
<li>Required versions or cutdowns</li>
<li>Whether the content will run as paid advertising</li>
<li>Distribution markets and languages</li>
</ul>
<p>A concept designed for a six-minute integration may not work as a 20-second paid vertical ad without substantial adaptation.</p>
<h4>Mandatory creative elements</h4>
<p>Clearly label what must appear:</p>
<ul>
<li>Product shots</li>
<li>Logo or packaging</li>
<li>Spoken brand name</li>
<li>Required talking points</li>
<li>On-screen disclosure</li>
<li>Call to action</li>
<li>Discount code or URL</li>
<li><a href="/glossary/hashtags/">Hashtags</a> and tags</li>
<li>Safety demonstrations</li>
<li>Copyright-cleared music or assets</li>
</ul>
<p>Separate <strong>must-haves</strong> from <strong>ideas</strong>. When every suggestion is presented as mandatory, the creator cannot prioritize the message.</p>
<h4>Creative boundaries</h4>
<p>List prohibited claims, competitor restrictions, unsafe demonstrations, visual conflicts, sensitive topics, or brand-safety concerns. Explain the reason when possible so the creator can propose a better alternative.</p>
<h4>References</h4>
<p>Examples can communicate pacing, framing, humor, graphics, or level of polish. They should be labeled according to what the team should learn from them. A reference is not permission to copy another creator’s work.</p>
<h4>Review and revision process</h4>
<p>Name the approver, deadline, file format, and included revision rounds. Feedback should be consolidated and tied to the agreed brief.</p>
<p>“Make it pop” is not actionable. “The product name is incorrect at 0:24, and the offer must say ‘up to 20%’ rather than ‘20%’” is actionable.</p>

<h3>Creative brief vs. campaign brief</h3>
<p>The names are often used interchangeably, and there is no universal industry rule. A practical separation is:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Campaign brief:</strong> The overall plan—business goal, audience, offer, channels, <a href="/glossary/deliverables/">deliverables</a>, schedule, and success metrics.</li>
<li><strong>Creative brief:</strong> The communication plan for the content—message, tone, concept, required elements, examples, and execution constraints.</li>
</ul>
<p>For a multi-creator launch, everyone may receive one campaign brief while each creator or content format receives a tailored creative brief.</p>
<h3>Creative brief vs. script</h3>
<p>A brief should not automatically dictate every word. It may include approved wording for a legal disclosure, product name, offer, or regulated claim, but it normally leaves room for the creator’s authentic delivery.</p>
<p>A script can be appropriate for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Precise product instructions</li>
<li>Legal or safety wording</li>
<li>Narrated animation</li>
<li>A tightly timed advertisement</li>
<li>A <a href="/glossary/voice-over/">voice-over</a> sequence</li>
<li>Multi-language production</li>
</ul>
<p>Even then, the creator should be able to identify lines that are inaccurate, unnatural, or inconsistent with real experience.</p>
<h3>How creators should review a creative brief</h3>
<p>Before approving a concept, check:</p>
<ol>
<li>Is the product information accurate and current?</li>
<li>Can the creator honestly support the required opinion?</li>
<li>Are mandatory and optional elements clearly separated?</li>
<li>Does the format fit the creator’s normal content?</li>
<li>Are the disclosure instructions clear?</li>
<li>Are copyrighted music, logos, footage, and claims cleared?</li>
<li>Are revisions and approval deadlines realistic?</li>
<li>Does the requested content match the signed deliverables?</li>
<li>Are paid-media versions or raw assets included in the fee?</li>
<li>Will the call to action still be valid on publication day?</li>
</ol>
<p>When the brief expands the workload or rights beyond the agreement, the creator should request a written change and revised compensation.</p>
<h3>How brands can preserve creator authenticity</h3>
<ul>
<li>Define the outcome rather than scripting every joke.</li>
<li>Provide facts, evidence, and boundaries early.</li>
<li>Let the creator recommend the format that fits the audience.</li>
<li>Avoid requiring praise before the creator has tested the product.</li>
<li>Consolidate feedback through one decision-maker.</li>
<li>Review for accuracy and compliance, not personal taste alone.</li>
<li>Allow the creator to disclose the partnership clearly.</li>
<li>Use performance lessons to improve the next brief rather than imitating one <a href="/glossary/viral/">viral</a> video.</li>
</ul>
<p>Creative freedom does not mean the brand gives up every requirement. It means the brief focuses control on the facts and risks that matter while letting the creator solve the communication problem.</p>
<h3>Common creative-brief mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>No single message:</strong> The content becomes a list of unrelated features.</li>
<li><strong>Too many mandatory points:</strong> The creator rushes through a script instead of telling a coherent story.</li>
<li><strong>Vague tone words:</strong> Stakeholders interpret “premium,” “viral,” or “authentic” differently.</li>
<li><strong>Copying a reference:</strong> The brand asks for originality while demanding another creator’s exact execution.</li>
<li><strong>Unsupported claims:</strong> The brief includes performance, health, financial, or environmental claims without proof.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring platform context:</strong> Text and calls to action are hidden by captions or mobile controls.</li>
<li><strong>Confusing feedback with expansion:</strong> New deliverables appear during revisions.</li>
<li><strong>No version control:</strong> Different stakeholders review different files.</li>
<li><strong>Late product changes:</strong> The offer or feature changes after filming.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Who writes a creative brief?</h4>
<p>A marketer, creative strategist, agency, producer, or creator-partnerships manager often prepares the first version. Product, legal, media, and the creator may contribute information.</p>
<h4>How long should a creative brief be?</h4>
<p>It should be as short as possible while remaining complete. One or two pages may be enough for a straightforward asset, while a complex regulated campaign may need appendices.</p>
<h4>Is a creative brief a contract?</h4>
<p>No, not by itself. It guides creative work. Payment, ownership, usage, exclusivity, cancellation, and liability should be addressed in a contract or statement of work.</p>
<h4>Can creators change the creative brief?</h4>
<p>They can propose changes, and collaboration often improves the concept. Material changes should be approved in writing before production.</p>
<h4>Does a creative brief eliminate revisions?</h4>
<p>No, but a clear brief reduces subjective surprises and gives both sides an agreed standard for evaluating the work.</p>
