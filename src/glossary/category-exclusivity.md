<h2>Category Exclusivity</h2>
<p><strong>Category exclusivity</strong> is a contract term that restricts a creator from promoting or working with competing brands in a defined product or service category for a specified period.</p>
<p>For example, a meal-delivery sponsor might prohibit the creator from accepting another paid meal-kit sponsorship for 30 days before and 30 days after the sponsored video publishes. The restriction should identify what counts as the category, which competitors are covered, where the restriction applies, and how long it lasts.</p>
<p>Category exclusivity is not automatically part of every <a href="/glossary/sponsorship/">sponsorship</a>. It must be negotiated and written into the agreement.</p>
<h3>Why brands request category exclusivity</h3>
<p>A sponsor may want exclusivity to:</p>
<ul>
<li>Prevent competing endorsements from appearing close together</li>
<li>Make the creator's recommendation feel more credible</li>
<li>Protect a launch or seasonal campaign</li>
<li>Avoid paying to build attention that immediately benefits a competitor</li>
<li>Secure a clear association between the creator and the sponsor</li>
<li>Support a longer-term ambassador relationship</li>
</ul>
<p>For the creator, exclusivity has an opportunity cost because it can block other paid work. That cost should be considered when using a <a href="/glossary/creator-rate-card/">creator rate card</a> or preparing the campaign quote.</p>
<h3>What a category exclusivity clause should define</h3>
<h4>Product or service category</h4>
<p>Avoid broad labels such as “technology,” “finance,” “food,” or “beauty” unless the creator understands exactly what they cover.</p>
<p>A narrower definition might be:</p>
<ul>
<li>Consumer tax-preparation software</li>
<li>Meal-kit delivery subscriptions</li>
<li>Wireless noise-canceling headphones</li>
<li>Direct-to-consumer mattresses</li>
<li>Cloud-based video editing software</li>
<li>Ready-to-drink energy beverages</li>
</ul>
<p>The creator should check whether the restriction includes adjacent products that do not directly compete.</p>
<h4>Named competitors</h4>
<p>A specific competitor list is often easier to apply than an undefined phrase such as “all competing brands.” The agreement should also explain whether the sponsor can add new competitors later and whether acquisitions, parent companies, and subsidiaries count.</p>
<h4>Time period</h4>
<p>Define the start and end dates. The restriction may include:</p>
<ul>
<li>A pre-publication blackout period</li>
<li>The publication date</li>
<li>A post-publication blackout period</li>
<li>The full campaign term</li>
<li>A longer ambassador term</li>
</ul>
<p>“Thirty days” should identify whether it means calendar days, business days, or a specific date range.</p>
<h4>Platforms and content types</h4>
<p>Clarify whether exclusivity applies to:</p>
<ul>
<li>YouTube videos</li>
<li>YouTube Shorts</li>
<li>Livestreams</li>
<li>Community posts</li>
<li>Other social platforms</li>
<li>Podcasts or newsletters</li>
<li>Public appearances</li>
<li>Unposted content made for a brand</li>
<li>Affiliate links and codes</li>
<li>Organic mentions that are not paid</li>
</ul>
<p>A YouTube sponsorship should not automatically block every type of work on every platform unless the agreement says so and the price reflects the restriction.</p>
<h4>Geography and audience</h4>
<p>Some deals limit exclusivity to a country, language, or campaign market. A global restriction is broader than a U.S.-only restriction.</p>
<h4>Existing obligations</h4>
<p>List current sponsorships, ambassador deals, affiliate programs, scheduled content, and long-standing organic brand relationships as exceptions before signing.</p>

<p>Category exclusivity is not the same as usage rights or ownership. A brand can restrict competitors without owning the video, and it can license the video without restricting the creator's other sponsorships.</p>
<h3>How category exclusivity affects creator pricing</h3>
<p>Exclusivity can increase the fee because the creator may lose future revenue or need to reject existing opportunities. The impact depends on:</p>
<ul>
<li>Length of the blackout period</li>
<li>Breadth of the category</li>
<li>Number and size of competitors</li>
<li>Number of platforms covered</li>
<li>Whether organic and affiliate mentions are restricted</li>
<li>The creator's normal deal frequency in that category</li>
<li>Seasonality and upcoming launches</li>
<li>Whether the sponsor wants renewal options</li>
<li>Whether the restriction continues after the content is removed</li>
</ul>
<p>There is no universal percentage premium that fits every deal. Current creator-platform guidance acknowledges that exclusivity raises flat fees but does not set one required rate.</p>
<h3>Carveouts creators can negotiate</h3>
<p>A creator may request exceptions for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Existing signed agreements</li>
<li>Existing affiliate links and evergreen descriptions</li>
<li>Unpaid editorial commentary</li>
<li>Product comparisons and news coverage</li>
<li>Products personally purchased before the deal</li>
<li>Brands outside a named competitor list</li>
<li>Different product lines owned by the same parent company</li>
<li>Unposted production work that the creator does not endorse</li>
<li>Incidental products appearing in the background</li>
<li>Previously published content that remains online</li>
<li>Opportunities approved by the sponsor in writing</li>
</ul>
<p>The contract should also explain what happens if a competitor approaches the creator during the restricted period. The creator may be allowed to schedule the deal after the blackout or request a written waiver.</p>
<h3>Red flags in an exclusivity clause</h3>
<p>Review carefully when a clause:</p>
<ul>
<li>Uses a category broader than the sponsor's actual business</li>
<li>Has no end date</li>
<li>Applies worldwide without a campaign reason</li>
<li>Restricts unpaid speech, reviews, or ordinary product use</li>
<li>Allows the sponsor to change the competitor list unilaterally</li>
<li>Covers parent companies and every subsidiary without naming them</li>
<li>Continues after cancellation regardless of payment</li>
<li>Blocks existing deals that were disclosed before signing</li>
<li>Provides no additional compensation for a major restriction</li>
<li>Conflicts with another contract</li>
</ul>
<p>A creator may want legal advice before accepting a broad or long-term restriction.</p>
<h3>Category exclusivity in the campaign workflow</h3>
<p>Discuss exclusivity before finalizing the <a href="/glossary/deliverables/">deliverables</a> and price. It should not appear for the first time during the <a href="/glossary/approval-process/">approval process</a> after the creator has already produced the content.</p>
<p>The signed terms should match the <a href="/glossary/campaign-brief/">campaign brief</a>, scheduling plan, and any statements made during negotiation.</p>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Is category exclusivity the same as a non-compete?</h4>
<p>It is a limited type of competitive restriction, but it is normally narrower than a full non-compete. It should apply only to defined brands, categories, activities, platforms, and dates.</p>
<h4>Should creators charge more for category exclusivity?</h4>
<p>Often yes, because the restriction can block other revenue. The appropriate amount depends on the opportunity cost and scope; there is no universal premium.</p>
<h4>Does exclusivity include unpaid product mentions?</h4>
<p>Only if the agreement says it does. Creators should negotiate whether organic editorial content, comparisons, old videos, and personally purchased products are included or carved out.</p>
<h4>Can a brand extend the exclusivity period?</h4>
<p>Only according to the agreement or a new written amendment. Renewal options should identify notice, compensation, and the new end date.</p>
