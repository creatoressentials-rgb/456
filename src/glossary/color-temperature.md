<h2>Color Temperature</h2>
<p><strong>Color temperature</strong> describes whether a light source appears warmer and more orange or cooler and more blue. It is measured in <strong>kelvin</strong>, written with a number followed by K, such as 3200 K or 5600 K.</p>
<p>For video creators, color temperature matters because cameras do not automatically interpret the color of light exactly as human vision does. A mismatched white-balance setting can make skin tones look orange, blue, or inconsistent between shots.</p>

<p>These values are approximations. A light's actual output can vary with the fixture, dimming method, weather, time of day, reflections, and manufacturing tolerances.</p>
<h3>Color temperature versus <a href="/glossary/white-balance/">white balance</a></h3>
<p>These terms are connected but describe different things.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Color temperature</strong> describes the color appearance of the light illuminating the scene.</li>
<li><strong>White balance</strong> is the camera's compensation for that light.</li>
</ul>
<p>If a room is lit by warm 3200 K tungsten fixtures, setting the camera near 3200 K tells it to neutralize that warmth so white objects appear white. If the same camera stays at 3200 K under cooler daylight, the image can look blue.</p>
<p>The camera setting can seem to work backward from the light-source scale. Under unchanged lighting:</p>
<ul>
<li>Raising the camera's Kelvin white-balance number usually makes the recorded image warmer.</li>
<li>Lowering the camera's Kelvin setting usually makes the recorded image cooler.</li>
</ul>
<p>This happens because the setting tells the camera what kind of source it should compensate for. It does not turn the camera into a light emitting that color temperature.</p>

<p>For a continuous talking-head video, locked Kelvin or custom white balance usually creates more consistent color than auto white balance. Auto can visibly shift when a bright screen, colored object, or person enters the frame.</p>
<h3>A practical white-balance workflow</h3>
<ol>
<li>Turn on all lights that will be used in the final recording.</li>
<li>Eliminate or control unwanted light sources where possible.</li>
<li>Place a neutral gray or white reference at the subject position.</li>
<li>Set a preset, enter a Kelvin value, or create a custom white balance.</li>
<li>Check skin tone and neutral objects on a reliable monitor.</li>
<li>Match every camera before recording a multi-camera production.</li>
<li>Lock the setting so it does not change during the take.</li>
<li>Record a short reference clip for post-production.</li>
</ol>
<p>A white card must be lit by the same light that illuminates the subject. Holding it next to the camera can give the wrong result when the subject is standing under different lighting.</p>
<h3>Mixed color temperatures</h3>
<p>Mixed lighting occurs when sources with different color temperatures illuminate the same scene. Common examples include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Daylight from a window mixed with warm ceiling bulbs.</li>
<li>A daylight LED key light mixed with a warm practical lamp.</li>
<li>Stage lighting mixed with colored screens or signs.</li>
<li>Different brands of LED panels set to the same printed Kelvin value.</li>
</ul>
<p>One white-balance setting cannot perfectly neutralize two very different sources at the same time. A creator can solve or use the mixture intentionally.</p>
<p>Possible approaches include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Turn off one source.</li>
<li>Block window light or replace room bulbs.</li>
<li>Match adjustable fixtures to a common temperature.</li>
<li>Add color-correction gel to a light.</li>
<li>Position the subject so one source becomes dominant.</li>
<li>Keep a warm background practical while balancing accurately for the face.</li>
</ul>
<p>Mixed color can create depth and atmosphere when deliberate. It looks like an error when one side of a face is orange and the other is blue without a visual reason.</p>
<h3>Kelvin does not measure every color problem</h3>
<p>Color temperature mainly describes the orange-to-blue axis. It does not fully describe green or magenta tint.</p>
<p>Fluorescent and inexpensive LED fixtures can introduce a green cast even when their Kelvin label seems correct. Cameras often provide a separate white-balance shift, tint adjustment, or green-magenta control for this reason.</p>
<p>Kelvin also does not measure color-<a href="/glossary/rendering/">rendering</a> quality. Two lights can both claim 5600 K while rendering skin, fabric, and saturated colors differently because their spectral output differs. Metrics such as CRI, TLCI, SSI, and spectral measurements address other parts of light quality.</p>
<h3>Color temperature for a consistent creator setup</h3>
<p>A repeatable studio is easier to manage when the creator chooses one lighting standard.</p>
<ul>
<li>Set adjustable key and fill lights to the same target.</li>
<li>Replace mismatched room bulbs or turn them off.</li>
<li>Save the camera's Kelvin and tint settings.</li>
<li>Mark light intensity and position.</li>
<li>Keep window daylight controlled with curtains when consistency matters.</li>
<li>Record a neutral reference whenever the setup changes.</li>
</ul>
<p>Consistency matters more than copying another creator's exact number. A camera set to 5000 K can look excellent under a properly matched 5000 K setup. It can look wrong under an unknown mixture of window light and household bulbs.</p>
<h3>Creative white balance</h3>
<p>White balance does not always need to make the scene perfectly neutral. Creators can use a deliberate mismatch for mood.</p>
<ul>
<li>Set the camera slightly higher than the source temperature to retain warmth.</li>
<li>Set it slightly lower to create a cooler atmosphere.</li>
<li>Balance for the subject while allowing background lights to remain colored.</li>
<li>Use contrasting warm and cool sources to separate foreground and background.</li>
</ul>
<p>The key is intent and consistency. A deliberate warm image should remain stable from shot to shot rather than changing because auto white balance reacted unpredictably.</p>
<h3>White balance, log footage, and post-production</h3>
<p>Recording log or raw footage provides more grading flexibility, but it does not make white balance irrelevant.</p>
<p>Severe white-balance errors can push channels unevenly, complicate skin-tone correction, increase noise after adjustment, or make multiple cameras harder to match. Compressed video formats provide less room for large corrections than raw formats.</p>
<p>Creators should get white balance reasonably close in camera, capture a neutral reference, and then refine the creative look in post. <a href="/glossary/color-grading/">Color grading</a> is stronger when it begins with consistent source footage.</p>
<h3>Common color-temperature mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Thinking higher Kelvin always means a warmer light source.</li>
<li>Confusing the source temperature with the camera's compensation setting.</li>
<li>Leaving auto white balance active during a long take.</li>
<li>Mixing daylight and tungsten accidentally.</li>
<li>Assuming every fixture labeled 5600 K matches perfectly.</li>
<li>Using Kelvin to diagnose a green or magenta problem.</li>
<li>Expecting log footage to fix any white-balance error without consequences.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Is 3200 K warm or cool light?</h4>
<p>A 3200 K light source is considered warm relative to daylight. It has a more yellow-orange appearance. A camera set to 3200 K compensates for that warm source so neutral objects can appear neutral.</p>
<h4>Is 5600 K always the correct daylight white balance?</h4>
<p>No. It is a common production reference for daylight-balanced lighting, but actual daylight changes with time, weather, shade, altitude, and reflected surroundings. Use 5600 K as a starting point and evaluate the real scene.</p>
<h4>Why does increasing my camera Kelvin setting make the image warmer?</h4>
<p>The camera setting describes the light it is compensating for. A higher setting tells the camera to correct for a cooler source, so it adds warmer compensation to the recorded image.</p>
<h4>Should I use auto white balance for YouTube videos?</h4>
<p>Auto white balance is convenient for rapidly changing situations. For a controlled talking-head, product, or multi-camera recording, a locked Kelvin or custom setting is usually more consistent and avoids visible color shifts during the shot.</p>
