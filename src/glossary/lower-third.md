<h2>Lower Third</h2>
<p>A <strong>lower third</strong> is a text or graphic overlay placed in the lower portion of a video frame. It commonly identifies a person, role, location, subject, product, source, social handle, or call to action without replacing the main picture.</p>
<p>Despite the name, a lower third does not have to fill exactly one-third of the screen. The term describes its general placement. A design may be a small name label in a corner, a two-line banner, an animated social prompt, or a wider information strip.</p>
<p>Lower thirds can be static or animated. Animated versions are a common type of <a href="/glossary/motion-graphics/">motion graphics</a> and may use <a href="/glossary/keyframes/">keyframes</a> to control position, opacity, scale, masks, and timing.</p>
<h3>What information appears in a lower third?</h3>
<p>A lower third should answer a useful question quickly. Common examples include:</p>
<ul>
<li>A guest's name and job title</li>
<li>A creator's name, channel, or social handle</li>
<li>A location, date, event, or chapter topic</li>
<li>A product name, model, price, or specification</li>
<li>A quoted source or attribution</li>
<li>A website, discount code, or call to action</li>
<li>A score, statistic, or live update</li>
<li>A subscribe, like, or notification prompt</li>
</ul>
<p>The graphic may contain one line or several levels of information. The most important text should have the strongest visual hierarchy.</p>

<p>A lower third should not be used as a substitute for accessible captions. Captions follow speech and sound; a lower third provides identification or additional context.</p>
<h3>Why creators use lower thirds</h3>
<h4>To identify people without interrupting the video</h4>
<p>In interviews, documentaries, podcasts, testimonials, and collaborations, a lower third can show a person's name and role while the conversation continues.</p>
<p>A common two-line structure uses the name as the primary line and the title, organization, or context as the secondary line.</p>
<h4>To clarify topics and transitions</h4>
<p>A lower third can introduce a new section, label a step, summarize a key point, or show a definition without cutting to a separate title card.</p>
<p>Used sparingly, these graphics help viewers scan and remember information.</p>
<h4>To support product and tutorial content</h4>
<p>Reviews and tutorials can label models, settings, ingredients, tools, shortcuts, measurements, prices, or links. The graphic should remain on screen long enough to read but disappear before it becomes visual clutter.</p>
<h4>To reinforce branding</h4>
<p>Consistent typefaces, spacing, motion, and graphic shapes make lower thirds part of a channel's visual identity. A reusable system is usually more effective than redesigning every label from scratch.</p>
<h4>To prompt an action</h4>
<p>Creators often use lower-thirds for subscribe prompts, social handles, newsletter links, discount codes, or reminders. These should support the content rather than cover it or appear so frequently that viewers ignore them.</p>

<p>Animation should help the graphic enter, organize information, or leave cleanly. Movement that does not improve communication can compete with the speaker or subject.</p>
<h3>How to design an effective lower third</h3>
<h4>Keep the text concise</h4>
<p>Use the shortest wording that preserves meaning. Long biographies, URLs, and explanations are difficult to read while the video continues.</p>
<h4>Create a clear hierarchy</h4>
<p>Make the most important information larger, brighter, bolder, or otherwise more prominent. Secondary details should remain readable without competing with the name or topic.</p>
<h4>Protect contrast and readability</h4>
<p>Text must remain readable over changing footage. Solutions include a solid or translucent background shape, subtle shadow, outline, blur, gradient, or carefully selected placement.</p>
<p>A graphic that works over one frame may disappear over a brighter or busier frame later in the shot, so review the entire duration.</p>
<h4>Respect safe areas and interfaces</h4>
<p>Traditional title-safe guides help prevent text from being cropped on televisions and some displays. Online video adds different concerns: captions, playback controls, channel branding, Shorts interface buttons, and mobile screen edges can cover graphics.</p>
<p>Check the final design in the actual destination format, especially when adapting horizontal video to vertical, Shorts, Reels, or other vertical layouts.</p>
<h4>Avoid covering important content</h4>
<p>Move the lower third when it blocks a face, hands, a product, demonstration, gameplay HUD, captions, or another essential detail. "Lower third" is a convention, not a requirement to ignore the picture.</p>
<h4>Give viewers enough reading time</h4>
<p>The required duration depends on word count, familiarity, and motion. Read the text naturally while previewing the sequence, then allow a little extra time rather than using a universal number of seconds.</p>
<h3>Lower-third animation workflow</h3>
<p>A basic animated lower third can be built with these steps:</p>
<ol>
<li>Add text and any supporting shape or logo.</li>
<li>Place the design within the intended safe area.</li>
<li>Set the final resting position and visual hierarchy.</li>
<li>Create entrance <a href="/glossary/keyframes/">keyframes</a> for position, opacity, scale, or masks.</li>
<li>Add easing so the motion accelerates and settles naturally.</li>
<li>Hold the graphic long enough to read.</li>
<li>Animate or cut it off cleanly.</li>
<li>Save the design as a reusable template when appropriate.</li>
</ol>
<p>Motion Graphics templates can let an editor change names, colors, text, and timing without rebuilding the animation for every video.</p>
<h3>Common lower-third mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Too much text:</strong> The viewer cannot read the graphic while following the video.</li>
<li><strong>Weak contrast:</strong> Text disappears over the footage.</li>
<li><strong>Covering captions:</strong> Identification graphics compete with accessibility text.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring mobile layouts:</strong> Platform controls cover important details.</li>
<li><strong>Overly long animation:</strong> The entrance takes longer than the information deserves.</li>
<li><strong>Inconsistent styling:</strong> Each graphic uses different fonts, spacing, or motion.</li>
<li><strong>Leaving it up too long:</strong> The lower third becomes permanent clutter.</li>
<li><strong>Calling every title a lower third:</strong> Full-screen titles and centrally placed callouts are different elements.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Why is it called a lower third?</h4>
<p>The term comes from placing identification graphics in the lower region of the picture. It does not require the graphic to occupy exactly one-third of the frame.</p>
<h4>Is a lower third the same as a caption?</h4>
<p>No. Captions represent speech and meaningful audio. Lower thirds identify people, places, topics, products, or other contextual information.</p>
<h4>Can a lower third be on the right side?</h4>
<p>Yes. It can appear on either side or across the lower frame, provided it remains readable and does not cover important content or interface elements.</p>
<h4>Should lower thirds be animated?</h4>
<p>Not necessarily. A static lower third can be effective. Animation is useful when it directs attention or reinforces branding without distracting from the content.</p>
<h4>How long should a lower third stay on screen?</h4>
<p>Long enough for the intended viewer to read comfortably. The correct time depends on text length, complexity, motion, and the pace of the video.</p>
