<h2>Exclusivity Clause</h2>
<p>An <strong>exclusivity clause</strong> is a contract provision that limits a creator, brand, or both from entering certain relationships or using specified rights outside the deal.</p>
<p>In creator agreements, the clause may restrict competing sponsorships, work with named brands, publication on other platforms, licensing of the same asset, or other commercial activity for a defined time.</p>
<p>“Exclusive” is incomplete by itself. The contract should identify <strong>what is exclusive, to whom, where, and for how long</strong>.</p>
<h3>Common types of creator exclusivity</h3>
<h4><a href="/glossary/category-exclusivity/">Category exclusivity</a></h4>
<p>The creator cannot promote other brands within a defined product or service category.</p>
<p>For example, the contract may prohibit competing meal-kit sponsorships for a specified period. See <a href="/glossary/category-exclusivity">category exclusivity</a> for the detailed category-based restriction.</p>
<h4>Named-competitor exclusivity</h4>
<p>The agreement lists specific competing brands the creator cannot work with. This can be more precise than a broad category, but the contract should explain whether parent companies, subsidiaries, new products, and acquired brands are covered.</p>
<h4>Full brand exclusivity</h4>
<p>The creator agrees not to accept most or all other sponsorships during the term. This is substantially broader than category exclusivity and can block unrelated revenue.</p>
<h4>Platform exclusivity</h4>
<p>Content must appear only on one platform, or the creator cannot publish equivalent campaign content elsewhere.</p>
<p>A YouTube-exclusive video does not automatically restrict the creator's entire business unless the agreement says so.</p>
<h4>Format exclusivity</h4>
<p>The restriction applies only to defined content formats, such as dedicated videos, livestream integrations, Shorts, podcasts, or newsletter placements.</p>
<h4>Talent or appearance exclusivity</h4>
<p>The creator cannot appear in certain advertisements, events, or campaigns for other brands.</p>
<h4>Exclusive content license</h4>
<p>A brand receives exclusive <a href="/glossary/content-licensing">content licensing</a> rights in specified copyright uses. This is legally and commercially different from restricting which sponsors the creator may accept.</p>
<h3>What an exclusivity clause should define</h3>
<p>A usable clause should state:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Restricted party:</strong> Creator, brand, agency, or both</li>
<li><strong>Covered brands:</strong> Named competitors or an objective method for identifying them</li>
<li><strong>Category:</strong> The precise products or services included</li>
<li><strong>Activities:</strong> Sponsored posts, affiliate links, appearances, consulting, licensing, or organic mentions</li>
<li><strong>Formats:</strong> Videos, Shorts, livestreams, social posts, podcasts, newsletters, events, or other media</li>
<li><strong>Platforms:</strong> YouTube only or all channels</li>
<li><strong>Territory:</strong> Country, region, language market, or global reach</li>
<li><strong>Start date:</strong> Contract signing, production, first publication, or another event</li>
<li><strong>End date:</strong> A fixed date or measurable period</li>
<li><strong>Pre- and post-campaign window:</strong> Any blackout before and after publication</li>
<li><strong>Exceptions:</strong> Existing deals, old content, affiliates, editorial commentary, and approved relationships</li>
<li><strong>Approval or waiver process:</strong> How the creator can request permission for a new opportunity</li>
<li><strong>Compensation:</strong> Fee for the restriction</li>
<li><strong>Remedy:</strong> What happens if either party breaches the clause</li>
</ul>

<h3>How exclusivity affects pricing</h3>
<p>Exclusivity can increase the creator's fee because it can cause the creator to reject other work. The economic impact depends on:</p>
<ul>
<li>Breadth of the category</li>
<li>Number and size of blocked competitors</li>
<li>Duration</li>
<li>Platforms covered</li>
<li>Territory</li>
<li>Whether unpaid mentions are restricted</li>
<li>Whether existing affiliate income is affected</li>
<li>Campaign season and expected demand</li>
<li>Whether the creator normally works often in the category</li>
<li>Whether the sponsor receives an exclusive content license as well</li>
<li>Renewal options and hold periods</li>
</ul>
<p>There is no universal percentage that fits every agreement. A seven-day restriction against three named competitors is not equivalent to a one-year global restriction covering an entire industry.</p>
<h3>Carveouts to consider</h3>
<p>Creators can negotiate exceptions for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Existing signed brand deals</li>
<li>Existing evergreen affiliate links</li>
<li>Previously published videos</li>
<li>Unpaid editorial reviews and comparisons</li>
<li>News reporting or commentary</li>
<li>Products the creator personally purchased</li>
<li>Brands outside a named list</li>
<li>Separate product categories owned by the same parent company</li>
<li>Work performed without public endorsement</li>
<li>Background product appearances</li>
<li>Charitable or community partnerships</li>
<li>Opportunities approved in writing</li>
<li>Campaigns already disclosed during negotiation</li>
</ul>
<p>Each carveout should be written into the contract. An informal email may not override a signed agreement unless the contract permits that form of amendment.</p>
<h3>Exclusivity and content rights</h3>
<p>A creator can retain <a href="/glossary/content-ownership">content ownership</a> while accepting a sponsor exclusivity restriction.</p>
<p>The reverse is also possible: a creator may grant a brand an exclusive license to one asset while remaining free to work with competing sponsors on unrelated content.</p>
<p>The contract should not combine these concepts casually. Check for separate provisions covering:</p>
<ul>
<li>Ownership</li>
<li>Organic usage</li>
<li>Paid media</li>
<li>Derivative works</li>
<li>Sublicensing</li>
<li>Creator identity</li>
<li>Competitor restrictions</li>
<li>Platform restrictions</li>
<li>Portfolio rights</li>
</ul>
<h3>Exclusivity red flags</h3>
<p>Review the clause carefully when it:</p>
<ul>
<li>Uses undefined terms such as “competitor” or “similar business”</li>
<li>Covers every brand in a broad industry</li>
<li>Has no firm end date</li>
<li>Starts before the creator is paid or the campaign is confirmed</li>
<li>Continues after brand cancellation without compensation</li>
<li>Applies across all platforms for one small deliverable</li>
<li>Blocks existing contracts or affiliate programs</li>
<li>Lets the brand add competitors unilaterally</li>
<li>Includes parent companies and all subsidiaries without a list</li>
<li>Restricts honest editorial commentary</li>
<li>Gives the brand both perpetual exclusive content rights and broad creator exclusivity</li>
<li>Provides no fee for a material opportunity cost</li>
</ul>
<p>Enforceability can depend on governing law and the specific wording. This glossary entry is general educational information, not legal advice.</p>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Is an exclusivity clause the same as category exclusivity?</h4>
<p>No. Category exclusivity is one type of exclusivity clause. The broader clause may instead restrict platforms, formats, named competitors, <a href="/glossary/content-licensing/">content licensing</a>, appearances, or all brand partnerships.</p>
<h4>Should creators charge extra for exclusivity?</h4>
<p>Often yes when it blocks realistic revenue opportunities. The amount should reflect the actual scope and opportunity cost rather than a universal percentage.</p>
<h4>Can exclusivity apply to old videos?</h4>
<p>Only if the agreement covers them. Creators should carve out previously published content, old descriptions, and existing affiliate links when appropriate.</p>
<h4>Can a brand add competitors after signing?</h4>
<p>Only if the agreement gives it that right or the creator agrees to an amendment. An open-ended list can materially expand the restriction and should be reviewed carefully.</p>
