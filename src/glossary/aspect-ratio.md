<h2>Aspect Ratio</h2>
<p><strong>Aspect ratio</strong> is the proportional relationship between the width and height of an image or video frame. It is usually written as two numbers separated by a colon, such as 16:9, 9:16, or 1:1.</p>
<p>The first number represents width and the second represents height. A 16:9 frame is wider than it is tall. A 9:16 frame is taller than it is wide. A 1:1 frame is square.</p>
<h3>Aspect ratio is shape, not resolution</h3>
<p>Aspect ratio describes the frame's shape. <strong>Resolution</strong> describes the number of pixels inside that shape.</p>

<p>A creator can increase resolution without changing aspect ratio. Exporting 1920×1080 footage as 3840×2160 keeps the 16:9 shape. Changing 16:9 footage into 9:16 changes the frame itself and usually requires cropping, reframing, or adding space.</p>

<p>No ratio is automatically more professional than another. The right choice depends on where the video will be viewed, how the subject moves, and whether the creator needs room for graphics, captions, products, or multiple people.</p>
<h3>How YouTube displays different aspect ratios</h3>
<p>YouTube's standard aspect ratio on a computer is 16:9. However, YouTube's player can adapt to videos with different ratios and to the viewer's device.</p>
<p>A vertical or square upload does not need to be placed inside a 16:9 canvas. YouTube may add interface padding when needed, especially when a tall video is viewed in a wide desktop browser. The platform recommends avoiding permanent black bars or padding inside the uploaded file because baked-in borders interfere with the player's ability to adapt dynamically.</p>
<p>This means a creator should generally export the actual intended frame shape:</p>
<ul>
<li>Export horizontal content as a true 16:9 or other chosen horizontal frame.</li>
<li>Export vertical content as a true portrait frame such as 9:16.</li>
<li>Avoid embedding a narrow video inside a larger black rectangle just to reach a standard resolution.</li>
</ul>

<p>Saying "vertical video" describes the general orientation. Saying "9:16" identifies a specific vertical shape. A 4:5 video is also vertical, but it is not as tall and narrow as 9:16.</p>
<h3>What happens when the project ratio changes</h3>
<p>Changing aspect ratio does not simply make the same image larger or smaller. It changes which parts of the original frame can fit.</p>
<p>A 16:9 shot converted to 9:16 has three common options:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Crop the sides.</strong> This fills the portrait frame but can remove people, text, or background details.</li>
<li><strong>Scale the full image down.</strong> This preserves everything but leaves empty space above and below or uses a designed background.</li>
<li><strong>Reframe dynamically.</strong> The editor moves the crop over time to follow the speaker or action.</li>
</ol>
<p>Automatic reframing tools can speed up this process, but creators should still review every cut. A tool may follow a face while cutting off a product, hand gesture, screen recording, or on-screen label.</p>
<h3>Planning one shoot for horizontal and vertical video</h3>
<p>Creators who want both long-form and vertical edits should plan before recording.</p>
<ul>
<li>Keep the important subject near the center when possible.</li>
<li>Leave space around faces and products for alternate crops.</li>
<li>Record a wider master shot than the final horizontal composition requires.</li>
<li>Capture separate close-ups and vertical takes for key moments.</li>
<li>Keep text, logos, and captions away from edges that may be cropped or covered by platform controls.</li>
<li>Use higher-resolution source footage when reframing will require significant cropping.</li>
</ul>
<p>A centered composition is easier to repurpose, but it should not make every shot visually flat. Important horizontal scenes may still need a dedicated vertical version rather than a compromised one-size-fits-all frame.</p>
<h3>Aspect ratio and <a href="/glossary/youtube-thumbnails/">thumbnails</a></h3>
<p>The video frame and the thumbnail are separate assets. A creator can upload a vertical video while still needing a thumbnail or promotional graphic designed for another shape in search, channel, or feed surfaces.</p>
<p>Do not assume that text placed near the edge of a 9:16 edit will remain readable everywhere the video is previewed. Interfaces can crop previews or overlay buttons, titles, captions, and engagement controls. Keep critical text inside a conservative central area and test the result on actual devices.</p>
<h3>Letterboxing, pillarboxing, and cropping</h3>
<p>When a video and display have different shapes, the player or editor must decide how to fit them.</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
  <th>Treatment</th>
  <th>What happens</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
  <td>Letterboxing</td>
  <td>Bars appear above and below a wider image inside a taller frame</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Pillarboxing</td>
  <td>Bars appear on the left and right of a narrower image inside a wider frame</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Cropping</td>
  <td>Parts of the image are removed so the frame fills the display</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Stretching</td>
  <td>The image is distorted to fill a different shape</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Adaptive player sizing</td>
  <td>The player changes shape to better match the uploaded video</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>Stretching is usually the worst option because it changes the proportions of people and objects. Cropping can work well when it is intentional. Adaptive sizing is preferable when the platform supports it.</p>
<h3>Choosing an aspect ratio</h3>
<p>Ask these questions before setting the <a href="/glossary/timeline/">timeline</a>:</p>
<ol>
<li>Where will the primary audience watch the finished video?</li>
<li>Is the content mainly a person, a product, gameplay, a screen recording, or a wide environment?</li>
<li>Does the shot require room for two people or side-by-side visuals?</li>
<li>Will the same footage be repurposed into Shorts or vertical social clips?</li>
<li>Are captions and graphics being designed for the chosen frame?</li>
<li>Is the camera recording enough resolution to support later cropping?</li>
</ol>
<p>For a normal YouTube upload, 16:9 remains the safest default. For full-screen mobile viewing, 9:16 is usually the better starting point. A creator should choose deliberately rather than exporting every project in the ratio used by the recording device.</p>
<h3>Common aspect ratio mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Calling 1080p an aspect ratio instead of a resolution label.</li>
<li>Reversing 16:9 and 9:16.</li>
<li>Baking black bars into the exported video unnecessarily.</li>
<li>Stretching a clip instead of cropping or reframing it.</li>
<li>Recording a tight horizontal composition with no room for a vertical crop.</li>
<li>Placing captions or text against edges covered by platform controls.</li>
<li>Assuming one crop will work equally well for every platform and device.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>What aspect ratio should I use for YouTube?</h4>
<p>Use 16:9 for a standard horizontal YouTube video. Use 9:16 for a full-screen vertical video. YouTube can display other ratios and automatically adapts its player, so creators do not need to force every upload into a 16:9 canvas.</p>
<h4>Is 1920×1080 an aspect ratio?</h4>
<p>No. It is a resolution. Its width and height simplify to a 16:9 aspect ratio. Other resolutions, including 1280×720 and 3840×2160, share the same 16:9 shape.</p>
<h4>Does changing aspect ratio lower video quality?</h4>
<p>The ratio itself does not determine quality. However, cropping and enlarging a small portion of the original frame can reduce effective resolution. Recording at a higher resolution gives editors more room to reframe.</p>
<h4>Should I add black bars to a vertical YouTube video?</h4>
<p>Usually not. Upload the true vertical frame. YouTube's player can adapt and add its own padding where needed. Permanent bars inside the file can create an unnecessarily small image or double padding on some devices.</p>
