<h2>Cost per Acquisition</h2>
<p><strong>Cost per acquisition</strong>, or <strong>CPA</strong>, is the average amount spent to acquire a defined customer or completed business action.</p>
<p>A common formula is:</p>
<p><strong>CPA = total campaign cost ÷ number of qualifying acquisitions</strong></p>
<p>If a brand spends $5,000 and acquires 100 new paying customers, its campaign CPA is $50.</p>
<p>The definition of <strong>acquisition</strong> must be written clearly. It might mean a first purchase, paid subscription, qualified lead, app install, or another action.</p>
<h3>Cost per acquisition vs. cost per action</h3>
<p>Google Ads commonly uses <strong>CPA</strong> to mean <strong>cost per action</strong>.</p>
<p>In broader marketing language:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
  <th>Term</th>
  <th>Common meaning</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
  <td>Cost per action</td>
  <td>Cost for any defined conversion, such as a lead, install, or purchase</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Cost per acquisition</td>
  <td>Cost to acquire a customer or commercially meaningful account</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Cost per lead</td>
  <td>Cost for a qualified or unqualified lead, depending on the definition</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Cost per install</td>
  <td>Cost for an app installation</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Cost per sale</td>
  <td>Cost for a completed purchase</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>The terms can overlap. A creator contract or report should define the action rather than relying only on the acronym CPA.</p>
<h3>CPA formula examples</h3>
<h4>Customer acquisition CPA</h4>
<ul>
<li>Total campaign cost: $12,000</li>
<li>New customers: 240</li>
<li>CPA: $50</li>
</ul>
<h4>Lead CPA</h4>
<ul>
<li>Total campaign cost: $3,000</li>
<li>Qualified leads: 100</li>
<li>Cost per qualified lead: $30</li>
</ul>
<h4>Purchase CPA</h4>
<ul>
<li>Total campaign cost: $8,000</li>
<li>Approved purchases: 160</li>
<li>Cost per purchase: $50</li>
</ul>
<p>These are not interchangeable outcomes. A $30 lead CPA cannot be compared directly with a $50 customer CPA without knowing lead quality and close rate.</p>
<h3>What costs belong in CPA?</h3>
<p>The answer depends on the report.</p>
<h4>Media CPA</h4>
<p>Uses advertising spend only.</p>
<p><strong>Ad spend ÷ attributed acquisitions</strong></p>
<h4>Creator campaign CPA</h4>
<p>May include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Creator fee</li>
<li><a href="/glossary/paid-usage">Paid usage</a> fee</li>
<li>Whitelisting fee</li>
<li>Media spend</li>
<li>Agency cost</li>
<li>Production</li>
<li>Affiliate commission</li>
<li>Discount subsidy</li>
<li>Tracking platform</li>
</ul>
<h4>Fully loaded customer acquisition cost</h4>
<p>May also include broader sales and marketing expenses such as salaries, software, creative production, and overhead.</p>
<p>A dashboard labeled CPA may use only media spend while the finance team calculates a broader acquisition cost.</p>
<h3>CPA vs. affiliate commission</h3>
<p>An <a href="/glossary/affiliate-commission">affiliate commission</a> is the amount paid to the affiliate for an approved action.</p>
<p>CPA is the advertiser's average cost per acquisition.</p>
<p>They can be equal when:</p>
<ul>
<li>Commission is the only campaign cost</li>
<li>Every approved conversion earns the same commission</li>
</ul>
<p>They differ when the advertiser also pays:</p>
<ul>
<li>Creator fee</li>
<li>Advertising spend</li>
<li>Network fee</li>
<li>Discount</li>
<li>Agency fee</li>
<li>Production costs</li>
</ul>
<h3>CPA vs. conversion rate</h3>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
  <th>CPA</th>
  <th><a href="/glossary/conversion-rate">Conversion rate</a></th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
  <td>Measures cost efficiency</td>
  <td>Measures the share that converts</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Cost ÷ conversions</td>
  <td>Conversions ÷ eligible opportunities</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Lower can be better if quality is maintained</td>
  <td>Higher can be better if value is maintained</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Depends on spend</td>
  <td>Does not directly include spend</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>A campaign can have a strong conversion rate but high CPA when clicks are expensive. It can have a modest conversion rate but efficient CPA when traffic is inexpensive and qualified.</p>
<h3>Actual CPA vs. Target CPA</h3>
<h4>Actual or average CPA</h4>
<p>The observed average cost per recorded conversion.</p>
<p>Google calculates average CPA by dividing total conversion cost by total conversions.</p>
<h4>Target CPA</h4>
<p>A Smart Bidding target telling Google the average amount the advertiser wants to pay per conversion.</p>
<p>Target CPA does not mean:</p>
<ul>
<li>Every conversion will cost exactly that amount</li>
<li>The campaign will always hit the target</li>
<li>Conversion quality will remain constant</li>
<li>The target is profitable</li>
</ul>
<p>Google states that some conversions can cost more and others less while the bidding system attempts to achieve the target average.</p>
<h3>CPA and conversion quality</h3>
<p>A low CPA is not automatically good.</p>
<p>It can be misleading when:</p>
<ul>
<li>Leads are unqualified</li>
<li>Purchases are low-value</li>
<li>Customers refund</li>
<li>Fraud is high</li>
<li>Free trials never convert</li>
<li>Existing customers are counted as acquisitions</li>
<li>The campaign attracts one-time discount buyers</li>
<li>A micro-conversion is treated as a customer</li>
</ul>
<p>The best acquisition metric can incorporate:</p>
<ul>
<li>New-customer status</li>
<li>Gross margin</li>
<li>Refund rate</li>
<li>Retention</li>
<li>Customer lifetime value</li>
<li>Subscription activation</li>
<li>Lead-to-sale rate</li>
</ul>
<h3>CPA and customer lifetime value</h3>
<p>A business can often afford a higher CPA for a customer who produces greater long-term value.</p>
<p>Simplified comparison:</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
  <th>Customer</th>
  <th style="text-align:right">CPA</th>
  <th style="text-align:right">Gross profit over relationship</th>
  <th>Observation</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
  <td>A</td>
  <td style="text-align:right">$30</td>
  <td style="text-align:right">$20</td>
  <td>Unprofitable before overhead</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>B</td>
  <td style="text-align:right">$80</td>
  <td style="text-align:right">$300</td>
  <td>Potentially attractive</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>C</td>
  <td style="text-align:right">$50</td>
  <td style="text-align:right">$50</td>
  <td>Break-even before overhead</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>Revenue is not profit. The allowable CPA should reflect margins, service cost, returns, and cash flow.</p>
<h3>Creator campaign CPA</h3>
<p>A brand evaluating a sponsored video may calculate:</p>
<p><strong>Total creator campaign cost ÷ attributed new customers</strong></p>
<p>Total cost might include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Sponsorship compensation</li>
<li>Product cost</li>
<li>Shipping</li>
<li>Agency fee</li>
<li>Tracking</li>
<li>Discount</li>
<li>Paid amplification</li>
<li><a href="/glossary/partnership-ads">Partnership ads</a></li>
</ul>
<p>The creator should confirm which costs and acquisitions the brand is using before accepting a CPA-based performance requirement.</p>
<h3>CPA and attribution</h3>
<p>CPA depends on <a href="/glossary/attribution">attribution</a>.</p>
<p>If the final click receives all credit, a creator who built awareness may appear to have a high CPA or no acquisitions.</p>
<p>If a long view-through window credits many conversions, CPA may appear unusually low.</p>
<p>The campaign should define:</p>
<ul>
<li>Attribution model</li>
<li>Conversion window</li>
<li>Click vs. view credit</li>
<li>New-customer definition</li>
<li>Returns</li>
<li>Cross-device handling</li>
<li>Reporting source</li>
</ul>
<h3>CPA and conversion delay</h3>
<p>A customer may convert after the initial report date.</p>
<p>CPA can initially appear high because:</p>
<ul>
<li>Cost is recorded immediately</li>
<li>Conversions arrive later</li>
<li>Offline sales are imported later</li>
<li>Return windows delay approval</li>
</ul>
<p>Evaluate campaigns after enough time has passed for the conversion cycle.</p>
<h3>Improving CPA</h3>
<p>A brand can reduce CPA through:</p>
<ul>
<li>Better <a href="/glossary/audience-fit">audience fit</a></li>
<li>Stronger creative</li>
<li>More accurate targeting</li>
<li>Better landing page</li>
<li>Faster checkout</li>
<li>Better offer</li>
<li>Higher conversion rate</li>
<li>Lower media cost</li>
<li>Better product-market fit</li>
<li>Removing low-quality placements</li>
<li>Improving follow-up</li>
<li>Tracking the correct conversion</li>
</ul>
<p>Reducing CPA by selecting an easier but less valuable conversion can damage the business.</p>
<h3>CPA red flags</h3>
<p>Be cautious when:</p>
<ul>
<li>Acquisition is undefined</li>
<li>Existing customers are counted as new</li>
<li>Media CPA is presented as fully loaded acquisition cost</li>
<li>Refunds are ignored</li>
<li>Lead quality is omitted</li>
<li>Different attribution windows are compared</li>
<li>A target CPA is described as a guaranteed price</li>
<li>Cost excludes creator or agency fees</li>
<li>The campaign optimizes to a low-value event</li>
<li>Revenue rather than margin determines profitability</li>
<li>Small conversion counts produce unstable averages</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>What is the formula for CPA?</h4>
<p>Divide the defined campaign cost by the number of qualifying acquisitions or actions. Both parts of the formula must be defined.</p>
<h4>Is CPA cost per action or cost per acquisition?</h4>
<p>Google Ads commonly uses CPA for cost per action. Many marketers use cost per acquisition for customer acquisition. Reports should specify the conversion being counted.</p>
<h4>Is a lower CPA always better?</h4>
<p>No. A lower CPA can reflect low-quality leads, low-value buyers, existing customers, or an easier conversion action.</p>
<h4>Is Target CPA the amount charged for every conversion?</h4>
<p>No. It is an average bidding target. Individual conversions can cost more or less, and actual performance can differ from the target.</p>
