<h2>Headroom</h2>
<p><strong>Headroom</strong> is the visible space between the top of a person's head and the upper edge of the video frame. It is a basic framing consideration in portraits, interviews, talking-head videos, documentaries, livestreams, and narrative scenes.</p>
<p>Good headroom makes the subject feel intentionally placed. Too much can make the person appear low or disconnected from the frame, while too little can feel cramped unless the crop is a deliberate close-up.</p>

<h3>How Much Headroom Should You Leave?</h3>
<p>There is no universal measurement. Headroom changes with shot size, aspect ratio, subject movement, hairstyle, camera angle, and the intended mood.</p>

  <thead>
    <tr><th>Shot Type</th><th>Typical Headroom Approach</th><th>Why</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Wide or full-body shot</td><td>Enough space to show the environment without making the person look accidentally low</td><td>The setting is an important part of the composition</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Medium shot</td><td>A modest, visually balanced gap above the head</td><td>Keeps attention on face and gestures</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Medium close-up</td><td>Usually a small amount of space</td><td>The face and upper body dominate the frame</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Tight close-up</td><td>The top of the head may be cropped deliberately</td><td>Eyes and facial expression matter more than showing the entire head</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
<p>Nikon's video-composition guidance defines headroom as the amount of space above the subject's head and recommends avoiding excessive empty space. That is a useful starting principle, not a fixed pixel or percentage rule.</p>

<h3>Headroom and Eye Placement</h3>
<p>Headroom is closely connected to where the subject's eyes appear in the frame. In many portrait and <a href="/glossary/talking-head-video/">talking-head</a> compositions, the eyes sit near the upper-third area rather than the head being vertically centered.</p>
<p>Placing the eyes too low can create excessive empty space above the subject. Placing them too high can make the frame feel crowded or cut off important hair and movement.</p>

<h3>Headroom vs. Look Room</h3>

  <thead>
    <tr><th>Term</th><th>Space It Describes</th><th>Example</th></tr>

    <tr><td>Headroom</td><td>Space above the subject's head</td><td>The gap between hair and the top frame edge</td></tr>

  </tbody>

<h3>Can You Crop the Top of a Person's Head?</h3>
<p>Yes. A close-up can intentionally crop part of the hair or top of the head while keeping the eyes and expression prominent. Nikon specifically notes that a tight close-up can crop part of the head when the eyes remain well placed.</p>
<p>The problem is not the crop itself. The problem is an accidental or hesitant crop that looks like a framing mistake. A deliberate tight crop should support intimacy, intensity, detail, or graphic composition.</p>

<h3>Headroom for YouTube Creators</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Talking-head videos:</strong> Keep the eyes comfortably high while preserving space for normal posture changes.</li>
  <li><strong>Livestreams:</strong> Leave a little movement tolerance because creators lean, sit up, and turn during long sessions.</li>
  <li><strong>Interviews:</strong> Match headroom across subjects unless a visual difference is deliberate.</li>
  <li><strong>Standing presentations:</strong> Account for gestures, posture, and movement rather than framing only the starting position.</li>
  <li><strong>Vertical video:</strong> Recheck headroom after the crop because the subject may appear larger in the narrower frame.</li>
  <li><strong>Overlays:</strong> Leave room for platform titles or captions only when they will actually occupy that area.</li>
</ul>

<h3>How Camera Angle Affects Headroom</h3>
<p>A high or low camera viewpoint changes the visible shape of the background and the apparent space around the head. Tilting a camera downward can introduce more floor or desk behind the subject, while tilting upward may include more ceiling or sky.</p>
<p>Do not solve every headroom problem by tilting the camera. Adjust camera height, distance, lens, subject position, and frame intentionally so perspective remains appropriate.</p>

<h3>Headroom With Moving Subjects</h3>
<p>Movement changes the required safety margin. A seated presenter may rise, a child may bounce, or an interview subject may lean forward. When the camera is locked off, frame for the expected movement rather than the subject's exact position during a quiet test.</p>
<p>A camera operator can reframe a moving subject, but constant correction may distract viewers. Plan movement marks and practice the shot when possible.</p>

<h3>Matching Headroom Between Cameras</h3>
<p>In a multi-camera interview, noticeably different headroom can make cuts feel abrupt even when both frames are technically acceptable. Match eye position, subject scale, look room, and visual weight—not only the number of inches above the head.</p>
<p>A second camera may intentionally be tighter or more profile-oriented, but the difference should look designed rather than accidental.</p>

<h3>Common Headroom Mistakes</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Leaving a large empty area above the head because the face was centered vertically.</li>
  <li>Using one fixed headroom rule for every shot size.</li>
  <li>Avoiding all head crops, even in an intentional close-up.</li>
  <li>Framing a still subject perfectly but failing to allow for normal movement.</li>
  <li>Matching the top gap across cameras while ignoring different eye positions and subject sizes.</li>
  <li>Tilting the camera excessively instead of correcting camera height or placement.</li>
  <li>Confusing headroom with look room or lead room.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently Asked Questions</h3>
<h4>How many inches of headroom should a video have?</h4>
<p>Headroom is judged within the image, not by a real-world inch measurement. The correct amount depends on shot size, aspect ratio, subject movement, and visual intent.</p>

<h4>Is too little headroom always bad?</h4>
<p>No. Tight close-ups may crop the top of the head deliberately. The crop should look confident and preserve the important facial features.</p>

<h4>Should the head be centered vertically?</h4>
<p>Usually not in a standard portrait or talking-head frame. The eyes are commonly placed higher in the image, leaving less space above the head than below the face.</p>

<h4>Why does headroom change in vertical video?</h4>
<p>A vertical crop often increases the subject's relative size and removes side space. The framing should be reviewed rather than automatically applying the horizontal crop.</p>
