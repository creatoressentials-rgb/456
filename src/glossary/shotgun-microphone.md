<h2>Shotgun Microphone</h2>
<p>A <strong>shotgun microphone</strong> is a highly directional microphone that uses a narrow pickup pattern and an interference tube to reduce sound arriving from the sides. It is commonly used for film dialogue, interviews, documentaries, outdoor recording, sports, wildlife, and creator videos where the microphone needs to stay outside the frame.</p>
<p>A shotgun mic does not zoom into distant sound or make faraway voices louder by magic. It improves isolation by accepting the aimed source more strongly than much of the off-axis sound. Distance and placement still determine whether the recording sounds close and clear or distant and reverberant.</p>
<h3>How a shotgun microphone works</h3>
<p>Most shotgun microphones combine:</p>
<ol>
<li>A directional microphone capsule, often supercardioid or hypercardioid at lower frequencies.</li>
<li>A long slotted interference tube in front of the capsule.</li>
<li>Carefully spaced openings that admit sound from different positions along the tube.</li>
</ol>
<p>Sound arriving from directly in front follows similar path lengths and reaches the capsule in phase. Sound entering from the sides travels different distances through the tube. At many frequencies, those arrivals partially cancel one another.</p>
<p>The result is a narrow forward pickup region often described as <strong>lobar</strong>.</p>
<h3>What "lobar" means</h3>
<p>A shotgun's pattern is not a perfect cone that rejects everything outside it. The pattern changes with frequency.</p>
<ul>
<li>At higher frequencies, the interference tube can create a narrow forward lobe.</li>
<li>At lower frequencies, the capsule itself provides more of the directionality.</li>
<li>Side lobes and a rear pickup lobe may remain.</li>
<li>Off-axis sound can change tone rather than simply becoming quieter.</li>
</ul>
<p>Longer interference tubes can maintain stronger directionality to lower frequencies, but they also become larger, harder to position indoors, and potentially more sensitive to reflected sound arriving from complex angles.</p>
<h3>Shotgun microphone "reach" is a myth</h3>
<p>A shotgun microphone does not amplify a source based on distance. The voice still becomes quieter relative to room reflections and environmental noise as the microphone moves farther away.</p>
<p>What the mic can do is reject some competing sound from other directions. That may improve the signal-to-noise ratio, but it cannot replace close placement.</p>
<p>For a talking-head video, a shotgun boomed roughly just outside the frame can sound significantly better than the same microphone mounted on the camera across the room.</p>

<p>The best position is normally the closest location that stays outside the composition and maintains a clear aim at the speaker.</p>
<h3>How to aim a shotgun microphone</h3>
<p>Point the microphone toward the mouth, upper chest, or the region where the voice remains consistent as the person moves. The exact aim depends on the microphone, room, framing, and tone desired.</p>
<p>Useful placement practices include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Keep the capsule as close as the shot allows.</li>
<li>Aim the main axis directly at the speaker.</li>
<li>Avoid pointing the rear or side lobes toward a loud noise source.</li>
<li>Rehearse head turns and body movement.</li>
<li>Monitor with headphones from the recording device.</li>
<li>Use a stand or operator to follow moving speakers.</li>
<li>Keep the microphone and boom shadows out of the frame.</li>
</ul>
<p>A shotgun that is only a few inches off-axis can sound thinner or less natural than one accurately aimed.</p>
<h3>Shotgun microphones indoors</h3>
<p>Shotgun microphones can work indoors, but reflective rooms require care. Walls, ceilings, floors, windows, and tables send delayed copies of the voice toward the interference tube from many angles. The tube may cancel some frequencies more than others, creating hollow or colored dialogue.</p>
<p>A short hypercardioid or supercardioid microphone without a long interference tube is often preferred in small reflective rooms because its off-axis response may sound more natural.</p>
<p>That does not mean every indoor shotgun recording fails. Results improve when:</p>
<ul>
<li>The room is treated or softly furnished.</li>
<li>The microphone is short and has smooth off-axis response.</li>
<li>The mic is close to the speaker.</li>
<li>The ceiling and nearby surfaces are not highly reflective.</li>
<li>The operator tests the actual room rather than following a rigid rule.</li>
</ul>
<p>Moving the microphone closer usually helps more than buying a longer shotgun.</p>
<h3>Shotgun microphones outdoors</h3>
<p>Outdoor spaces often have fewer nearby reflections, making an interference tube effective for isolating dialogue. The main challenges become wind, traffic, crowds, handling noise, and changing distance.</p>
<p>Wind protection is essential. A bare microphone or thin foam cover is often insufficient in moving air.</p>

<p>Wind rumble can overload the capsule or preamp and may be impossible to remove cleanly in editing.</p>
<h3>Shock mounts and handling noise</h3>
<p>A sensitive directional microphone can pick up vibrations from a camera, stand, boom pole, cable, or operator. A shock mount suspends the mic to reduce mechanical noise.</p>
<p>Creators should also:</p>
<ul>
<li>Secure the cable so it does not tap the pole or mount.</li>
<li>Avoid gripping or rubbing the boom.</li>
<li>Keep stabilization motors and camera fans away from the mic.</li>
<li>Use low-cut filtering carefully when rumble is present.</li>
<li>Monitor during rehearsal, not only after recording.</li>
</ul>
<p>A low-cut filter can reduce some vibration and wind energy, but it cannot repair severe handling noise.</p>

<h3>Setting gain for a shotgun microphone</h3>
<p>Place and aim the mic before setting gain. Have the speaker rehearse at the loudest realistic level, then adjust the preamp so peaks remain below clipping with usable headroom.</p>
<p>If the recording is noisy:</p>
<ul>
<li>Move the microphone closer.</li>
<li>Reduce room or environmental noise.</li>
<li>Confirm that the mic has power.</li>
<li>Use a cleaner preamp or higher-output mic if necessary.</li>
<li>Avoid recording extremely low and boosting heavily later.</li>
</ul>
<p>More gain cannot separate a distant voice from room reflections because both are amplified together.</p>
<h3>Shotgun microphones for YouTube and creator videos</h3>
<p>A shotgun is a strong choice when:</p>
<ul>
<li>A visible desk microphone would distract from the shot.</li>
<li>The creator records seated or standing dialogue in a consistent position.</li>
<li>A boom can stay close above the frame.</li>
<li>Outdoor interviews require directional rejection.</li>
<li>The camera follows action and a compact on-camera mic is better than the built-in mic.</li>
<li>A second audio track is needed alongside wireless lavaliers.</li>
</ul>
<p>It is a weaker choice when the speaker is far from the camera, moves unpredictably without an operator, or records in a small untreated room with severe reflections.</p>
<h3>Common shotgun microphone mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Leaving the mic on the camera ten feet from the speaker.</li>
<li>Believing a longer tube provides magical reach.</li>
<li>Pointing the mic approximately rather than directly.</li>
<li>Ignoring rear and side lobes.</li>
<li>Using a long shotgun in a reflective room without testing.</li>
<li>Recording outdoors with only a foam windshield.</li>
<li>Skipping a shock mount on a camera or boom.</li>
<li>Turning on <a href="/glossary/phantom-power/">phantom power</a> without checking compatibility.</li>
<li>Setting gain before final placement.</li>
<li>Trying to remove severe echo or wind entirely in post-production.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>How far away can a shotgun microphone record clear dialogue?</h4>
<p>There is no fixed distance. Room reflections, background noise, microphone sensitivity, placement, voice level, and framing all matter. For polished dialogue, keep it as close as practical—usually just outside the frame rather than on a distant camera.</p>
<h4>Is a shotgun microphone good for indoor YouTube videos?</h4>
<p>It can be, especially when short, close, and used in a treated or softly furnished room. In a small reflective room, a hypercardioid pencil microphone may produce more natural off-axis sound.</p>
<h4>Does a shotgun microphone need phantom power?</h4>
<p>Many professional XLR condenser shotguns use 48V phantom power. Other models use batteries, rechargeable cells, plug-in power, or USB. Check the exact microphone rather than the category.</p>
<h4>Should I use a shotgun microphone or lavalier?</h4>
<p>Use a shotgun when you can place it close outside the frame and want natural dialogue without attaching a mic to clothing. Use a lavalier when the subject moves, the camera is far away, or a boom cannot remain close. Recording both provides redundancy.</p>
