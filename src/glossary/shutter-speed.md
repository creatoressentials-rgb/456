<h2>Shutter Speed</h2>
<p><strong>Shutter speed</strong> is the length of time a camera records light for a photograph or for each individual video frame. It is usually written in seconds or fractions of a second, such as 2 seconds, 1/50, 1/250, or 1/2000.</p>
<p>A slower shutter keeps the exposure active longer, recording more light and more motion blur. A faster shutter records for less time, reducing light and making moving subjects appear sharper or more frozen.</p>
<p>Shutter speed is one side of the <a href="/glossary/exposure-triangle/">exposure triangle</a>, alongside <a href="/glossary/aperture/">aperture</a> and <a href="/glossary/iso/">ISO</a>.</p>
<h3>How shutter speed works</h3>
<p>In a mechanical camera shutter, curtains uncover and then cover the sensor. In electronic capture, the sensor begins and ends exposure through electronic timing. The practical setting still describes exposure duration even when no physical shutter curtain moves.</p>

<h3>Fast vs. slow shutter speed</h3>
<p>A <strong>fast shutter speed</strong> can:</p>
<ul>
<li>Freeze sports, wildlife, water droplets, or fast gestures.</li>
<li>Reduce blur caused by camera movement.</li>
<li>Create crisp, staccato motion in video.</li>
<li>Require a wider aperture, higher ISO, or more lighting.</li>
</ul>
<p>A <strong>slow shutter speed</strong> can:</p>
<ul>
<li>Record more light.</li>
<li>Create motion trails or smooth moving water.</li>
<li>Make video motion feel softer or dreamier.</li>
<li>Increase blur from the subject and the camera.</li>
<li>Require a tripod, stabilization, or an <a href="/glossary/nd-filter/">ND filter</a> in bright conditions.</li>
</ul>
<p>Blur is not automatically a defect. It communicates movement and is a major part of how motion feels on screen.</p>
<h3>Shutter speed for video</h3>
<p>In video, shutter speed works together with <a href="/glossary/frame-rate-fps/">frame rate</a>. Each frame must be exposed during the available frame interval.</p>
<p>A common starting point is the <strong>180-degree shutter guideline</strong>, which uses an exposure time of approximately half the frame interval.</p>

<h3>Shutter speed vs. shutter angle</h3>
<p>Cinema cameras may express exposure as <strong>shutter angle</strong> instead of time.</p>
<p>A 360-degree shutter exposes for the entire frame interval. A 180-degree shutter exposes for half of it. A 90-degree shutter exposes for one-quarter.</p>
<p>The relationship is:</p>
<p><strong>Exposure time = shutter angle ÷ (360 × frame rate)</strong></p>

<h3>The 180-degree guideline is not a law</h3>
<p>Creators may intentionally choose a different shutter.</p>
<p>Use a faster shutter when:</p>
<ul>
<li>Recording sports or fast action for frame-by-frame clarity.</li>
<li>Planning to stabilize or track motion in post-production.</li>
<li>Creating a tense, sharp, documentary action style.</li>
<li>Capturing frames that may be used as still images.</li>
<li>Reducing motion blur for visual-effects tracking or keying.</li>
</ul>
<p>Use a slower shutter when:</p>
<ul>
<li>Creating dreamy motion trails.</li>
<li>Recording in very low light when blur is acceptable.</li>
<li>Emphasizing speed or chaos.</li>
<li>Producing intentional long-exposure effects.</li>
</ul>
<p>A shutter slower than the frame interval may cause some cameras to duplicate frames, lower the effective frame rate, or enter a special slow-shutter mode.</p>

<h3>Camera shake vs. subject motion</h3>
<p>Two different problems can blur an image:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Camera shake</strong> occurs because the camera moves during exposure.</li>
<li><strong>Subject motion</strong> occurs because the subject moves during exposure.</li>
</ul>
<p>A tripod can solve camera shake but does not freeze a moving person. Image stabilization can compensate for some camera rotation but cannot stop a dancer, athlete, pet, or vehicle.</p>
<p>The familiar reciprocal guideline—using a shutter around one over the focal length for handheld photos—is only a starting point. Crop factor, pixel density, stabilization, technique, and subject motion can require a faster or allow a slower setting.</p>
<h3>Shutter speed and exposure</h3>
<p>Changing shutter speed changes recorded light.</p>
<ul>
<li>Moving from 1/60 to 1/120 cuts exposure by one stop.</li>
<li>Moving from 1/250 to 1/125 adds one stop.</li>
<li>Moving from 1/1000 to 1/250 adds two stops.</li>
</ul>
<p>To maintain brightness after increasing shutter speed, a creator may:</p>
<ul>
<li>Open the <a href="/glossary/aperture/">aperture</a>.</li>
<li>Raise <a href="/glossary/iso/">ISO</a>.</li>
<li>Add more light.</li>
<li>Change the camera angle or time of day.</li>
</ul>
<p>For video, creators often lock shutter for motion appearance and control brightness with lighting, aperture, ISO, and an <a href="/glossary/nd-filter/">ND filter</a>.</p>
<h3>Shutter speed and artificial-light flicker</h3>
<p>LEDs, fluorescent lamps, signs, displays, and other electronic sources may pulse at a rate tied to electrical frequency or their internal driver. A mismatched shutter can produce:</p>
<ul>
<li>Brightness pulsing.</li>
<li>Dark horizontal bands.</li>
<li>Color shifts.</li>
<li>Uneven exposure across the frame.</li>
</ul>
<p>In a 60 Hz region, 1/60 or 1/120 often works well. In a 50 Hz region, 1/50 or 1/100 is often a safer starting point. Modern LED drivers and dimmers do not always follow simple mains timing, so test the actual fixture.</p>
<p>Some cameras offer variable shutter or synchro-scan controls that fine-tune exposure timing to remove bands.</p>

<h3>Shutter speed vs. rolling shutter</h3>
<p>These terms are related to time but describe different things.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Shutter speed</strong> is how long each pixel or frame gathers light.</li>
<li><strong>Rolling-shutter readout</strong> is how long the sensor takes to scan from one area to another.</li>
</ul>
<p>A fast shutter can make each scanned row sharper, but it does not remove the time difference between the top and bottom of a slow-reading sensor. Bent vertical lines, leaning buildings, and warped propellers are primarily readout issues.</p>
<h3>Shutter speed and flash</h3>
<p>Mechanical shutters have a maximum normal flash-sync speed, often around 1/160 to 1/250 depending on the camera. Faster settings can cause the shutter curtains to form a moving slit, preventing a standard flash burst from illuminating the entire frame at once.</p>
<p>High-speed sync pulses the flash repeatedly while the slit travels, allowing faster shutter settings at the cost of flash power. An <a href="/glossary/nd-filter/">ND filter</a> is another way to use a wide aperture in daylight while staying at normal sync speed.</p>
<p>Electronic and global shutters have different flash restrictions. Confirm the camera manual.</p>
<h3>Choosing shutter speed for creator videos</h3>
<ol>
<li>Select the delivery and recording <a href="/glossary/frame-rate-fps/">frame rate</a>.</li>
<li>Start near a 180-degree relationship.</li>
<li>Record a movement test with hands, face, and camera motion.</li>
<li>Increase shutter if cleaner action frames are more important than smooth blur.</li>
<li>Decrease it only when intentional blur or low-light needs justify the result.</li>
<li>Check artificial lights and screens for flicker.</li>
<li>Adjust brightness with aperture, ISO, lighting, or ND rather than changing shutter accidentally.</li>
<li>Review motion at normal playback speed, not only as a paused frame.</li>
</ol>
<p>The correct shutter is the one that creates the desired motion while remaining technically usable.</p>
<h3>Common shutter-speed mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Treating the 180-degree guideline as mandatory.</li>
<li>Leaving auto shutter active and allowing motion blur to change during a clip.</li>
<li>Using a fast still-photo shutter for normal dialogue video and creating choppy movement.</li>
<li>Slowing shutter below the frame interval without understanding frame duplication.</li>
<li>Confusing rolling-shutter distortion with motion blur.</li>
<li>Assuming stabilization freezes a moving subject.</li>
<li>Ignoring LED flicker and screen refresh rates.</li>
<li>Changing shutter to fix bright daylight instead of using ND.</li>
<li>Forgetting flash-sync limits in photography.</li>
<li>Judging video motion only from individual paused frames.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>What shutter speed should I use for 30 fps video?</h4>
<p>Start near 1/60 second for a conventional 180-degree motion look. Use a faster shutter for crisper action or a slower one for intentional blur, while checking exposure and flicker.</p>
<h4>Is shutter speed the same as frame rate?</h4>
<p>No. Frame rate is how many frames are recorded each second. Shutter speed is how long each frame is exposed. The two settings work together to determine motion appearance.</p>
<h4>Does a faster shutter speed improve video quality?</h4>
<p>Not automatically. It can reduce motion blur and help tracking or action clarity, but it also captures less light and may make normal movement look harsh or stuttery.</p>
<h4>Why does my video flicker at the correct shutter speed?</h4>
<p>The light source may use an LED driver, dimmer, sign controller, or screen refresh rate that does not match the usual 50 or 60 Hz guideline. Adjust variable shutter or synchro scan while monitoring the actual fixture.</p>
