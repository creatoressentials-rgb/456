<h2>Video Framing</h2>
<p><strong>Video framing</strong> is the deliberate selection and arrangement of everything visible within the edges of a video image. It determines what the audience sees, what is excluded, how large the subject appears, and how visual elements relate to one another.</p>
<p>Framing can direct attention, reveal context, create balance, reserve space for graphics, and influence how close or distant a subject feels. It is one part of composition, alongside lighting, color, depth, movement, focus, and visual design.</p>

<h3>What Does Video Framing Control?</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Subject placement:</strong> Where people, products, and important objects sit within the image</li>
  <li><strong>Shot size:</strong> How much of the subject and surrounding environment are visible</li>
  <li><strong>Headroom and look room:</strong> The space above a person and in the direction they face</li>
  <li><strong>Background:</strong> Which environmental details are included or excluded</li>
  <li><strong>Positive and negative space:</strong> The balance between occupied and unoccupied areas</li>
  <li><strong>Foreground and depth:</strong> Whether objects near the camera add layers, context, or distraction</li>
  <li><strong>Aspect-ratio safety:</strong> Whether important content survives horizontal, square, or vertical crops</li>
</ul>

<h3>Framing vs. Composition</h3>
<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Term</th><th>Meaning</th><th>Example</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Frame</td><td>The visible rectangular image and its boundaries</td><td>Everything shown inside a 16:9 video image</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Framing</td><td>Choosing what appears inside those boundaries</td><td>Placing a presenter on the left and excluding a distracting doorway</td></tr>

  </tbody>

<p>Framing is a central part of composition, but the terms are not perfectly interchangeable. Composition describes the complete visual organization, while framing emphasizes the image boundaries and what is included within them.</p>

<h3>Framing vs. Shot Size vs. Camera Angle</h3>
<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Decision</th><th>Question It Answers</th><th>Example</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Framing</td><td>What is inside the image, and where is it placed?</td><td>A host on one side with room for text</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Shot size</td><td>How much of the subject or scene is visible?</td><td>Wide, medium, or close-up</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Camera angle</td><td>From what vertical or tilted viewpoint is the subject seen?</td><td>Eye level, high angle, low angle, or Dutch angle</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Camera movement</td><td>How does the viewpoint change during the shot?</td><td>Pan, tilt, push-in, or tracking movement</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
<p>A medium shot can be framed centrally or off-center and filmed from eye level or a low angle. These choices work together but describe different properties of the shot.</p>

<h3>Common Framing Approaches</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Centered framing:</strong> Places the subject near the center for symmetry, directness, or stability.</li>
  <li><strong>Thirds-based framing:</strong> Uses the <a href="/glossary/rule-of-thirds/">rule of thirds</a> as a guide for subject placement.</li>
  <li><strong>Tight framing:</strong> Leaves little visible space around the main subject.</li>
  <li><strong>Loose framing:</strong> Includes more surrounding space and environmental context.</li>
  <li><strong>Frame within a frame:</strong> Uses doors, windows, shelves, or foreground objects to surround the subject visually.</li>
  <li><strong>Layered framing:</strong> Uses foreground, subject, and background elements to create depth.</li>
  <li><strong>Negative-space framing:</strong> Intentionally leaves open space for mood, movement, text, or another visual element.</li>
</ul>

<h3>How to Frame a Person</h3>
<ol>
  <li>Choose a shot size based on whether the viewer needs facial expression, gestures, body movement, or environmental context.</li>
  <li>Place the camera near the intended eye line unless another viewpoint is deliberate.</li>
  <li>Leave appropriate space above the head and in the direction of the subject's gaze or movement.</li>
  <li>Avoid accidental crops through major joints such as knees, ankles, wrists, and elbows.</li>
  <li>Check the background edges for poles, plants, shelves, or bright objects that appear to grow from the subject.</li>
  <li>Decide whether the subject should be centered or offset for graphics, demonstrations, or visual direction.</li>
  <li>Confirm that focus, exposure, and lighting support the intended point of attention.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Framing for YouTube Videos</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong><a href="/glossary/talking-head-video/">Talking-head videos:</a></strong> Keep the eyes in a comfortable upper-frame position and leave room for gestures.</li>
  <li><strong>Product demonstrations:</strong> Make hands, controls, and important actions clearly visible.</li>
  <li><strong><a href="/glossary/screen-recording/">Screen recordings:</a></strong> Reserve space for a camera box without covering interface controls.</li>
  <li><strong>Interviews:</strong> Leave look room toward the interviewer and maintain compatible eye lines between angles.</li>
  <li><strong>Vertical clips:</strong> Keep essential subjects near a crop-safe central area when repurposing horizontal footage.</li>
  <li><strong>Thumbnails:</strong> Capture clear expressions and uncomplicated negative space for readable text.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Does the Lens Determine the Framing?</h3>
<p>The lens affects field of view and perspective, but it does not independently determine the final framing. A creator can move the camera, zoom, change lenses, crop, or reposition the subject to achieve different compositions.</p>
<p>However, moving the camera and changing focal length are not visually identical. Camera distance changes perspective and the relative size of foreground and background elements, while cropping or zooming from the same position primarily changes how much of the scene is included.</p>

<h3>Reframing During a Shot</h3>
<p><strong>Reframing</strong> is a small adjustment to preserve or improve composition as a subject moves. It may involve a short pan, tilt, or repositioning of a handheld camera.</p>
<p>Reframing should feel motivated rather than like the operator is constantly correcting mistakes. Leave movement space in advance when the subject's action is predictable.</p>

<h3>Cropping in Post vs. Framing in Camera</h3>
<p>Editing can tighten, reposition, or straighten a shot when the source resolution provides enough room. Cropping cannot recreate a missing background, change the original camera perspective, or restore body parts and objects that were never recorded.</p>
<p>Capture intentional framing during production and use post-production crops as an additional creative option rather than the only plan.</p>

<h3>Common Video-Framing Mistakes</h3>
<ul>
  <li>Leaving accidental empty space without a visual purpose.</li>
  <li>Ignoring objects and bright areas near the frame edges.</li>
  <li>Cropping a person awkwardly at a major joint.</li>
  <li>Using a composition rule without considering the message or background.</li>
  <li>Placing every subject in the center even when movement or graphics need space.</li>
  <li>Recording so tightly that vertical or alternate crops become impossible.</li>
  <li>Assuming digital cropping later will reproduce the perspective of a different camera position.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently Asked Questions</h3>
<h4>Is framing the same as shot size?</h4>
<p>No. Shot size describes how much of the subject or environment is shown. Framing also includes placement, spacing, balance, background, and the relationship to the image edges.</p>

<h4>Should every video use the rule of thirds?</h4>
<p>No. It is one useful guide. Centered, symmetrical, tightly framed, or deliberately unbalanced compositions may communicate the idea more effectively.</p>

<h4>Can framing change during one shot?</h4>
<p>Yes. Camera movement, subject movement, zooming, and reframing can change the composition during a continuous shot.</p>

<h4>Why does framing look different after uploading?</h4>
<p>The platform, player, device orientation, captions, interface controls, or alternate aspect-ratio crop may cover or remove parts of the original image. Test important layouts on the intended viewing formats.</p>
