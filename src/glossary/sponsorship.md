<h2>Sponsorship</h2>
<p>A <strong>sponsorship</strong> is a commercial arrangement in which a brand or other third party provides money, products, services, access, travel, or another benefit to support a creator, piece of content, event, or ongoing series. In exchange, the creator normally agrees to provide specified exposure, content, services, or promotional value.</p>
<p>In everyday creator language, sponsorship is often used broadly enough to include a paid integration, a dedicated <a href="/glossary/sponsored-video/">sponsored video</a>, a livestream segment, an event partnership, an affiliate campaign, or a longer-term ambassador relationship.</p>
<p>YouTube uses a narrower technical distinction. Its Help Center describes <strong>sponsorships</strong> as content financed in whole or in part by a third party that generally promotes the third party's brand, message, or product without necessarily integrating it directly into the content. YouTube separately defines paid product placements and endorsements.</p>
<h3>How a creator sponsorship works</h3>
<p>A typical sponsorship moves through several stages:</p>
<ol>
<li>The brand contacts the creator or the creator pitches the brand.</li>
<li>Both sides discuss <a href="/glossary/audience-fit/">audience fit</a>, campaign goals, timing, and budget.</li>
<li>They define the <a href="/glossary/deliverables/">deliverables</a>, compensation, rights, disclosure, exclusivity, and <a href="/glossary/approval-process/">approval process</a>.</li>
<li>The creator develops the concept and produces the agreed content.</li>
<li>The brand reviews the work according to the contract.</li>
<li>The creator publishes or delivers the assets.</li>
<li>The creator supplies any agreed performance report, invoice, or post-campaign materials.</li>
</ol>
<p>The signed agreement should control the final obligations. A <a href="/glossary/campaign-brief/">campaign brief</a> or <a href="/glossary/creative-brief/">creative brief</a> can guide the work, but it should not quietly expand the deal beyond the negotiated scope.</p>
<h3>Common forms of sponsorship</h3>
<h4>Sponsored integration</h4>
<p>The sponsor receives a segment inside a broader video. The creator may explain the product, demonstrate it, deliver approved talking points, and include a call to action.</p>
<h4>Dedicated sponsored video</h4>
<p>The full video centers on the sponsor, product, service, or campaign. Dedicated content usually gives the brand more prominence and may require a different price, production plan, and review process than a short integration.</p>
<h4>Series or channel sponsorship</h4>
<p>A brand supports multiple videos, an entire series, or a recurring segment. The sponsor may receive repeated mentions, title placement, set branding, links, or category exclusivity.</p>
<h4>Event or travel sponsorship</h4>
<p>A brand pays for travel, lodging, access, production expenses, or event participation. Those benefits can create a material relationship even when the creator does not receive a separate cash fee.</p>
<h4>Product or service sponsorship</h4>
<p>The creator receives equipment, software, subscriptions, services, or other items in exchange for agreed exposure or content. A gift with no posting requirement can still create a connection that may need disclosure if the creator later endorses the product.</p>
<h4>Performance-based sponsorship</h4>
<p>Some compensation depends on affiliate sales, leads, downloads, sign-ups, or another measurable action. Performance compensation should be distinguished from guaranteed production and publishing fees.</p>

<p>The wording used by brands is not consistent. A contract labeled “sponsorship” may function like an endorsement, product placement, licensing agreement, or production-services deal. Creators should evaluate the actual obligations rather than relying on the heading.</p>
<h3>What a sponsorship agreement should define</h3>
<p>A clear sponsorship agreement should address:</p>
<ul>
<li>The exact content and services the creator will provide</li>
<li>Draft, publication, and reporting deadlines</li>
<li>Compensation, payment schedule, expenses, and invoicing</li>
<li>Included <a href="/glossary/revision-round/">revision rounds</a></li>
<li>Required talking points, prohibited claims, links, codes, and calls to action</li>
<li>The <a href="/glossary/sponsorship-disclosure/">sponsorship disclosure</a> required in each format</li>
<li>Who may approve the content and how long review takes</li>
<li><a href="/glossary/content-ownership/">Content ownership</a> and usage rights</li>
<li>Paid advertising or brand partner access</li>
<li><a href="/glossary/category-exclusivity/">Category exclusivity</a> and competitor restrictions</li>
<li>How long published content must remain live</li>
<li>Cancellation, rescheduling, kill fees, and termination</li>
<li>Performance reporting and whether any outcome is actually guaranteed</li>
</ul>
<p>A creator's <a href="/glossary/creator-rate-card/">rate card</a> may provide baseline pricing, but a sponsorship quote should reflect the full scope and the opportunity cost of rights or restrictions.</p>
<h3>Sponsorship disclosure on YouTube</h3>
<p>YouTube requires creators to tell the platform when a video contains a paid product placement, sponsorship, endorsement, or other applicable commercial relationship by selecting the paid-promotion declaration in <a href="/glossary/youtube-studio/">YouTube Studio</a>.</p>
<p>That platform declaration is separate from a creator's own viewer-facing disclosure. Depending on the audience and applicable law, the creator may need to clearly explain the relationship in the video, description, livestream, Short, or other content.</p>
<p>For U.S.-facing endorsements, creators should also understand the <a href="/glossary/ftc-sponsorship-disclosure/">FTC sponsorship disclosure</a> principles. The FTC focuses on whether a material connection is made clear in a way viewers are likely to notice and understand.</p>
<h3>Does sponsorship guarantee a positive review?</h3>
<p>No. A sponsorship can pay for production, access, or exposure, but an endorsement should still reflect the creator's honest experience. A contract should not require a creator to make a claim they do not believe or cannot support.</p>
<p>Creators should identify the difference between:</p>
<ul>
<li>Objective product facts supplied by the brand</li>
<li>Claims the brand has substantiation to make</li>
<li>The creator's personal experience</li>
<li>Predictions, guarantees, or performance claims that cannot be verified</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Is a sponsorship the same as a brand deal?</h4>
<p>A sponsorship is one type of brand deal, but brand deal is the broader phrase. A brand deal can also cover unposted production work, affiliate arrangements, appearances, licensing, consulting, or other commercial services.</p>
<h4>Does a free product count as sponsorship?</h4>
<p>It can. A free product, discount, trip, service, or other benefit can create a material brand relationship. Whether the arrangement is called a sponsorship depends on the agreement, but the connection may still need to be disclosed when the creator mentions or endorses the product.</p>
<h4>Can YouTube still place ads on a sponsored video?</h4>
<p>Yes. YouTube says it may continue serving platform ads on videos that contain paid promotions. The creator's sponsorship and YouTube's ad system are separate revenue arrangements.</p>
<h4>Does the sponsor own the video?</h4>
<p>Not automatically. Ownership and usage rights depend on the contract. A creator may retain copyright while granting the sponsor limited permission to repost or advertise the content for specified platforms and time periods.</p>
