<h2>Work Made for Hire</h2>
<p><strong>Work made for hire</strong> is a specific U.S. copyright-law category in which the hiring or commissioning party is treated as the author and initial copyright owner of the work.</p>
<p>It is not a general phrase meaning “work someone paid for.” A commissioned creator project qualifies only if it meets one of the legal tests.</p>
<p>The U.S. Copyright Office identifies two situations:</p>
<ol>
<li>An employee creates the work within the scope of employment.</li>
<li>A specially ordered or commissioned work falls within an eligible statutory category and the parties expressly agree in a signed writing that it is work made for hire.</li>
</ol>
<h3>Employee-created work</h3>
<p>A work can be made for hire when:</p>
<ul>
<li>The creator is legally an employee</li>
<li>The work is created within the scope of that employment</li>
</ul>
<p>Whether someone is an employee for copyright purposes depends on the relationship's facts under agency-law principles, not only the label in the contract.</p>
<p>Relevant factors can include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Control over how the work is created</li>
<li>Skill required</li>
<li>Who supplies tools and workspace</li>
<li>Length of the relationship</li>
<li>Ability to assign additional projects</li>
<li>Method of payment</li>
<li>Employee benefits</li>
<li>Tax treatment</li>
<li>Whether the hiring party operates the relevant business</li>
<li>Whether the creator can hire assistants</li>
</ul>
<p>An independent-contractor clause does not automatically determine the copyright result.</p>
<h3>Commissioned independent-contractor work</h3>
<p>For a specially ordered or commissioned work to qualify, the Copyright Office says all four criteria must be satisfied:</p>
<ol>
<li>The work falls within one of the nine eligible categories.</li>
<li>The commissioning party and creator have a written agreement.</li>
<li>The agreement expressly states that the work is considered a work made for hire.</li>
<li>All parties sign the agreement.</li>
</ol>
<p>If one requirement fails, the commissioned work is not a work made for hire under that route.</p>
<h3>The nine commissioned-work categories</h3>
<p>A commissioned work must be intended for use as:</p>
<ol>
<li>A contribution to a collective work</li>
<li>A part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work</li>
<li>A translation</li>
<li>A supplementary work</li>
<li>A compilation</li>
<li>An instructional text</li>
<li>A test</li>
<li>Answer material for a test</li>
<li>An atlas</li>
</ol>
<p>A creator video may potentially fit the category of a part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work, depending on the project and agreement. That does not mean every social-media post, photograph, logo, or standalone creative service automatically qualifies.</p>
<h3>Work made for hire vs. assignment and license</h3>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
  <th>Arrangement</th>
  <th>Initial author and owner</th>
  <th>What the brand receives</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
  <td>Employee work made for hire</td>
  <td>Employer, when created within scope of employment</td>
  <td>Copyright ownership from creation</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Commissioned work made for hire</td>
  <td>Commissioning party, if every statutory requirement is met</td>
  <td>Copyright ownership from creation</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Copyright assignment</td>
  <td>Creator normally begins as owner, then transfers rights</td>
  <td>Ownership of the assigned rights</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Exclusive license</td>
  <td>Creator grants exclusive rights within the license</td>
  <td>Ownership interest in the exclusive rights granted</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>Nonexclusive <a href="/glossary/usage-rights">usage rights</a></td>
  <td>Creator retains ownership</td>
  <td>Permission to use the work within the stated scope</td>
</tr>
<tr>
  <td>File delivery</td>
  <td>Depends on the copyright agreement</td>
  <td>Possession of a copy, not necessarily copyright</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>A contract may say, “work made for hire, and if it is not legally work made for hire, the creator assigns the copyright.” That backup assignment is legally distinct from the work-made-for-hire claim and should be evaluated separately.</p>
<h3>Why work-made-for-hire status matters</h3>
<h4>Authorship</h4>
<p>The hiring or commissioning party is legally treated as the author.</p>
<h4>Copyright ownership</h4>
<p>The hiring or commissioning party is the initial owner unless a signed agreement says otherwise.</p>
<h4>Copyright duration</h4>
<p>For current works made for hire, the Copyright Office states that protection lasts 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation, whichever expires first.</p>
<h4>Statutory termination</h4>
<p>Certain copyright grants made by authors can later be subject to statutory termination procedures. The Copyright Office says those termination provisions do not apply to works made for hire.</p>
<h4>Registration</h4>
<p>The hiring or commissioning party is identified as the author of a qualifying work made for hire.</p>
<h3>Does payment create work-made-for-hire status?</h3>
<p>No. Payment alone does not satisfy the legal test.</p>
<p>A brand can pay a creator for:</p>
<ul>
<li>Production services</li>
<li>Publication</li>
<li><a href="/glossary/deliverables">Deliverables</a></li>
<li><a href="/glossary/usage-rights">Usage rights</a></li>
<li>A copyright assignment</li>
<li>Exclusivity</li>
<li>Paid advertising access</li>
<li>Raw files</li>
</ul>
<p>None of those items automatically makes the project a work made for hire.</p>
<h3>Work made for hire and creator videos</h3>
<p>A brand may ask for work-made-for-hire language when it commissions:</p>
<ul>
<li>An advertisement</li>
<li>Product demonstration</li>
<li>Training video</li>
<li>Commercial</li>
<li>Animation</li>
<li>Documentary segment</li>
<li>Series contribution</li>
<li>Other audiovisual asset</li>
</ul>
<p>For an independent creator, the parties still need the signed agreement and eligible category. They should also define:</p>
<ul>
<li>The exact work covered</li>
<li>Whether creator-owned templates and background materials are excluded</li>
<li>Treatment of raw footage and project files</li>
<li>Third-party music and stock assets</li>
<li>Portfolio and credit rights</li>
<li>The creator's name, voice, and likeness</li>
<li>Whether the creator also publishes the content on their own channel</li>
<li>Payment and cancellation</li>
<li>Rights in rejected drafts</li>
</ul>
<h3>Work made for hire and sponsored channel content</h3>
<p>A normal <a href="/glossary/sponsored-video">sponsored video</a> published on the creator's own channel is often structured differently from a brand-owned production.</p>
<p>The creator may:</p>
<ul>
<li>Own the video</li>
<li>Grant the brand limited <a href="/glossary/content-licensing">content licensing</a></li>
<li>Give the brand <a href="/glossary/paid-usage">paid usage</a></li>
<li>Retain editorial and channel control</li>
<li>Keep the content live under agreed terms</li>
</ul>
<p>Work-made-for-hire language could instead make the brand the author and owner of the covered work. Creators should understand that this is more than an ordinary usage-rights add-on.</p>
<h3>Preexisting materials and third-party rights</h3>
<p>Even when the final work qualifies, the agreement should address:</p>
<ul>
<li>Creator templates</li>
<li>Existing footage</li>
<li>Fonts</li>
<li>Music</li>
<li>Stock media</li>
<li>Software</li>
<li>Presets</li>
<li>Logos</li>
<li>Guest contributions</li>
<li>Location and talent releases</li>
</ul>
<p>The creator cannot make third-party content into the brand's work made for hire merely by including it in the project.</p>
<h3>Work-made-for-hire red flags</h3>
<p>Review language that:</p>
<ul>
<li>Applies to every work the creator has ever made</li>
<li>Labels the creator an employee only for copyright ownership</li>
<li>Claims work-made-for-hire status without an eligible category</li>
<li>Transfers raw files and unrelated background materials automatically</li>
<li>Includes a broad backup assignment without explaining it</li>
<li>Gives up credit and portfolio rights without discussion</li>
<li>Transfers ownership before payment</li>
<li>Conflicts with the creator's publishing obligations</li>
<li>Claims rights in rejected concepts and unused footage</li>
<li>Uses work made for hire as a substitute for a defined scope of work</li>
</ul>
<p>This is a technical legal doctrine. Creators should obtain legal advice for significant ownership transfers.</p>

<h3>Frequently asked questions</h3>
<h4>Is every commissioned creator video work made for hire?</h4>
<p>No. Independent-contractor work must fit one of the nine statutory categories and satisfy the express signed-writing requirements.</p>
<h4>Does writing “work made for hire” in a contract make it valid?</h4>
<p>Not by itself. The underlying legal requirements must also be satisfied.</p>
<h4>Is work made for hire the same as a copyright assignment?</h4>
<p>No. A qualifying work made for hire treats the hiring party as author and initial owner. An assignment transfers rights that initially belonged to the creator.</p>
<h4>Can a creator keep portfolio rights?</h4>
<p>The parties can negotiate permission for portfolio, case-study, award, or <a href="/glossary/creator-media-kit">creator media kit</a> use even when the brand owns the work.</p>
