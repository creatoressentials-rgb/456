<h2>Copyright Checks</h2>
<p><strong>Copyright checks</strong> are YouTube's upload-stage screening for potential copyrighted content in a video. During the Checks step, YouTube searches the upload for material that may match reference content in its copyright systems.</p>
<p>If an issue is found, the creator can review the affected content and available options before publication. A clean result is not permanent copyright clearance and does not replace the creator's responsibility to have the necessary rights.</p>

<h3>How Does the Copyright Check Work?</h3>
<ol>
<li>The creator uploads a video to YouTube.</li>
<li>YouTube processes the file and runs copyright screening.</li>
<li>The system compares video or audio against available copyrighted reference material.</li>
<li>If a match is found, a Content ID claim or related restriction may appear.</li>
<li>The creator reviews the owner, segment, policy, and available actions.</li>
<li>The creator can resolve, accept, or dispute the issue when appropriate.</li>
</ol>
<p>Content ID automatically generates claims when an upload matches another work or segment in the Content ID system.</p>

<h3>What Can a Content ID Claim Do?</h3>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Rights-Holder Policy</th><th>Possible Effect</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Monetize</td><td>Ads may run and revenue may go to or be shared with the claimant</td></tr>
<tr><td>Block</td><td>The video may be unavailable in some or all countries</td></tr>
<tr><td>Track</td><td>The rights holder may monitor viewing statistics</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>The policy can vary by country or region. One video may be monetized in one location and blocked in another.</p>

<h3>Copyright Claim vs. Copyright Strike</h3>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Issue</th><th>How It Begins</th><th>Typical Impact</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td><a href="/glossary/content-id/">Content ID claim</a></td><td>Automated match in YouTube's Content ID system</td><td>Affects the video through monetization, tracking, or blocking and usually does not create a strike</td></tr>
<tr><td><a href="/glossary/copyright-strike/">Copyright strike</a></td><td>A valid copyright removal request causes the video to be removed</td><td>Affects the channel and can lead to feature restrictions or termination after repeated active strikes</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>A creator should not call every copyright notice a strike. The Studio notice should identify which process is involved.</p>

<h3>Are Copyright Check Results Final?</h3>
<p>No. YouTube explicitly warns that copyright-check results are not final. A video can later receive:</p>
<ul>
<li>A manual Content ID claim</li>
<li>A copyright removal request</li>
<li>A copyright strike after a valid removal request</li>
<li>A changed policy from an existing claimant</li>
<li>A new match after a rights holder adds reference material</li>
<li>A claim after the creator edits the video or settings</li>
</ul>
<p>"No issues found" means the current screening did not identify a match or restriction. It does not prove that the video contains no copyrighted work.</p>

<h3>What Can the Check Miss?</h3>
<ul>
<li>Works not represented in the matching database</li>
<li>Material a rights holder has not yet claimed</li>
<li>Rights governed by contracts or licenses YouTube cannot evaluate</li>
<li>Trademark, privacy, publicity, confidentiality, or other non-copyright issues</li>
<li>Whether a copyright exception legally applies</li>
<li>Future manual review and takedown requests</li>
</ul>

<h3>Can the Check Decide Fair Use?</h3>
<p>No. YouTube says courts ultimately decide fair use based on the facts of each case. An automated match can create a claim even when the uploader believes the use is commentary, criticism, teaching, reporting, parody, or another exception.</p>
<p>Giving credit, owning a purchased copy, disabling monetization, or adding a disclaimer does not automatically provide permission or create fair use.</p>

<h3>What Can You Do When a Claim Appears?</h3>
<p>Available actions depend on the claim and content. Options may include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Leave the claim in place</li>
<li>Trim the claimed segment</li>
<li>Replace claimed music</li>
<li>Mute claimed audio</li>
<li>Share revenue when an applicable option exists</li>
<li>Dispute the claim with a valid reason</li>
</ul>
<p>Before disputing, confirm that you own the rights, have permission, qualify for a copyright exception, or believe the match is an error.</p>

<h3>What Is Not a Valid Dispute Reason?</h3>
<p>YouTube specifically says these are not legitimate reasons by themselves:</p>
<ul>
<li>Giving credit to the copyright owner</li>
<li>Owning a physical or digital copy of the song or video</li>
<li>Choosing not to monetize the upload</li>
</ul>
<p>A dispute is a formal statement about rights or legal use, not a request for the claimant to be generous.</p>

<h3>Can a Dispute Lead to a Strike?</h3>
<p>A normal Content ID claim is not itself a strike. However, if a claimant rejects a dispute or appeal, the claimant may submit a copyright removal request. If YouTube determines that request is valid, the video is removed and the channel receives a strike.</p>
<p>Creators should understand the dispute and appeal stages before escalating a claim.</p>

<h3>Can You Publish While the Copyright Check Runs?</h3>
<p>Yes. YouTube allows creators to publish while checks continue. That can be risky for a scheduled launch because a later claim may block, monetize, or restrict the video after viewers have been notified.</p>
<p>When timing allows, upload early as private, unlisted, or scheduled and review the status before the public release.</p>

<h3>Copyright Check vs. Copyright Match Tool</h3>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Tool</th><th>Purpose</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Upload copyright check</td><td>Screens the creator's upload for potential copyrighted-content matches</td></tr>
<tr><td>Copyright Match Tool</td><td>Helps eligible copyright owners find copies or potential copies of their own content on YouTube</td></tr>
<tr><td>Copyright removal request</td><td>A legal request from a rights holder to remove allegedly infringing content</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>The tools operate from different sides of YouTube's copyright system and should not be treated as interchangeable.</p>

<h3>Recommended Copyright Workflow</h3>
<ol>
<li>Use original material or obtain appropriate permissions and licenses.</li>
<li>Keep license receipts, contracts, releases, and source records.</li>
<li>Upload early enough for screening.</li>
<li>Review every detected segment and claimant policy.</li>
<li>Confirm whether a license covers YouTube, monetization, territory, and duration.</li>
<li>Resolve simple music or clip issues before publication when practical.</li>
<li>Dispute only with a valid basis.</li>
<li>Monitor the video after publication for later claims or changes.</li>
</ol>

<h3>Common Copyright-Check Mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Treating a clean check as legal permission.</li>
<li>Calling a Content ID claim a copyright strike.</li>
<li>Assuming a few seconds of music can never be claimed.</li>
<li>Believing credit or a disclaimer prevents infringement.</li>
<li>Publishing immediately without reviewing a detected restriction.</li>
<li>Disputing a claim because the creator does not want to share revenue.</li>
<li>Confusing the uploader's copyright check with the Copyright Match Tool.</li>
<li>Assuming YouTube can decide fair use automatically.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently Asked Questions</h3>
<h4>Does a clean copyright check mean my video is safe?</h4>
<p>No. It means the current screening did not identify an issue. Future claims, removal requests, and legal disputes remain possible.</p>
<h4>Is a Content ID claim a strike?</h4>
<p>Usually not. A strike follows a valid copyright removal request and video removal.</p>
<h4>Can copyrighted music be claimed if I use only a few seconds?</h4>
<p>Yes. YouTube warns that even short uses can receive a Content ID claim.</p>
<h4>Can YouTube determine whether my use is fair use?</h4>
<p>No. Courts ultimately decide fair use based on the specific facts.</p>
