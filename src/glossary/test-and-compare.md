<h2>Test and Compare</h2>
<p><strong>Test and Compare</strong> is the name YouTube originally used for its native thumbnail-testing feature. It allowed eligible creators to compare up to three thumbnail options on the same video using real viewer behavior.</p>
<p>YouTube has since expanded the feature and now labels the current workflow <strong>A/B Testing</strong>. Eligible creators can test titles, thumbnails, or title-and-thumbnail combinations. Creators and older help pages may still use "Test and Compare" when referring to the native experiment system.</p>

<h3>Is Test and Compare Still Available?</h3>
<p>The underlying capability remains available, but YouTube's current Studio instructions use the label <strong>A/B Testing</strong>. The current feature includes three test modes:</p>
<ul>
<li>Title only</li>
<li>Thumbnail only</li>
<li>Title and thumbnail</li>
</ul>
<p>The older term remains especially common when discussing thumbnail experiments introduced before title testing became broadly available.</p>

<h3>Test and Compare vs. YouTube A/B Testing</h3>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Term</th><th>Original or Current Meaning</th><th>Available Variables</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Test and Compare</td><td>Earlier name associated with YouTube's native thumbnail-testing tool</td><td>Up to three thumbnails</td></tr>
<tr><td><a href="/glossary/youtube-a-b-testing/">YouTube A/B Testing</a></td><td>Current expanded YouTube Studio feature</td><td>Up to three titles, thumbnails, or title-thumbnail combinations</td></tr>
<tr><td>Third-party A/B testing</td><td>Testing conducted by an outside service</td><td>Varies by provider and method</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>It is accurate to describe Test and Compare as the earlier thumbnail-focused version or branding of YouTube's current native testing system. It is less accurate to describe the present feature as thumbnail-only.</p>

<h3>How Does the Current Test Work?</h3>
<ol>
<li>The creator opens an eligible video in desktop YouTube Studio.</li>
<li>The creator selects <strong>A/B Testing</strong> beneath the title or thumbnail area.</li>
<li>The creator chooses a title-only, thumbnail-only, or combined test.</li>
<li>Up to three variations are uploaded or entered.</li>
<li>YouTube shows the variations concurrently during the test period.</li>
<li>The system evaluates each variation using watch-time share.</li>
<li>A clear winner is applied to the video after the test.</li>
</ol>
<p>YouTube says tests can take a few days and should complete within two weeks.</p>

<h3>Why Does YouTube Use Watch-Time Share?</h3>
<p>YouTube does not choose the winner from click-through rate alone. The platform says titles and thumbnails should help viewers choose the correct video, not merely generate clicks.</p>
<p>A variation can produce more clicks but weaker watch time if the packaging creates the wrong expectation. YouTube therefore optimizes the native test for watch-time share, which combines the ability to attract a click with the viewing that follows.</p>

<h3>What Results Can a Test Produce?</h3>
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Result</th><th>Meaning</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Winner</td><td>One variation clearly outperformed the others on watch-time share with statistically significant results</td></tr>
<tr><td>Performed Same</td><td>The variations performed about the same without a meaningful winner</td></tr>
<tr><td>Inconclusive</td><td>The experiment did not find a strong statistical difference in engagement</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>If no clear winner exists, YouTube uses the first uploaded title or title-thumbnail combination as the default. The creator can still select another option manually.</p>

<h3>Current Eligibility Requirements</h3>
<ul>
<li>The feature is available in YouTube Studio on desktop.</li>
<li>The channel must have access to advanced features.</li>
<li>Shorts are not eligible.</li>
<li>Scheduled live streams and active Premieres are not eligible.</li>
<li>Archived live streams can be tested.</li>
<li>A Premiere can become eligible after it ends and becomes a long-form video.</li>
<li>Made for Kids, private, mature, and age-restricted videos are not eligible under the current rules.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Test and Compare vs. Sequential Thumbnail Changes</h3>
<p>Before native testing, creators often changed a thumbnail manually, waited, and compared performance with an earlier period. This is not equivalent to a concurrent controlled experiment because audience composition, traffic sources, recommendation volume, seasonality, and video age may change between periods.</p>
<p>YouTube says its native tests show variations concurrently. Some third-party tools use sequential rotation and may choose a different winner because they measure another metric or run during changing conditions.</p>

<h3>Why Start With Older Videos?</h3>
<p>YouTube recommends testing selected older videos first. During an experiment, some variations may perform worse than others, so beginning with older content can reduce the risk to a new video's important initial release period.</p>
<p>An older video still needs enough impressions to produce meaningful evidence. A nearly inactive video may end with an inconclusive result.</p>

<h3>How to Create Useful Variations</h3>
<ul>
<li>Test meaningful differences rather than nearly identical designs.</li>
<li>Keep each option accurate to the video.</li>
<li>Change one main concept when you want a cleaner learning objective.</li>
<li>Test complete title-thumbnail combinations when their ideas depend on each other.</li>
<li>Use high-resolution thumbnail files.</li>
<li>Avoid including one intentionally weak option.</li>
<li>Write down the hypothesis before beginning.</li>
<li>Apply lessons across several videos before declaring a channel-wide rule.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Can You Change the Video During the Test?</h3>
<p>YouTube says changing the title or thumbnail manually while the experiment is running automatically stops the test. The creator must restart a new test to continue experimenting.</p>
<p>Other significant changes to the video's audience or traffic can also make interpretation harder, even when they do not technically stop the experiment.</p>

<h3>What Is the Control Group?</h3>
<p>YouTube may reserve a small percentage of traffic as a control group. Those viewers see the default title and thumbnail and are excluded from the experiment calculations.</p>
<p>As a result, not every impression necessarily receives one of the active test variations.</p>

<h3>Common Test-and-Compare Mistakes</h3>
<ul>
<li>Assuming the current feature is still thumbnail-only.</li>
<li>Calling manual before-and-after thumbnail changes a true concurrent test.</li>
<li>Choosing a winner from CTR while ignoring watch-time share.</li>
<li>Testing variations that are too similar to distinguish.</li>
<li>Running the first experiment on a low-impression video.</li>
<li>Changing the title or thumbnail during the experiment.</li>
<li>Assuming an inconclusive result means the tool failed.</li>
<li>Copying the winning style onto every future video without further testing.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Frequently Asked Questions</h3>
<h4>Did YouTube rename Test and Compare?</h4>
<p>YouTube's current workflow is labeled A/B Testing and expands beyond the earlier thumbnail-focused Test and Compare tool.</p>
<h4>How many versions can be tested?</h4>
<p>Up to three titles, thumbnails, or title-thumbnail combinations.</p>
<h4>Does the highest CTR win?</h4>
<p>No. YouTube selects results using watch-time share.</p>
<h4>Can Test and Compare be used on Shorts?</h4>
<p>No. Shorts are not eligible for YouTube's current native A/B testing feature.</p>
